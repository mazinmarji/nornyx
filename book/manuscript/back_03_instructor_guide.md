# Instructor and Self-Study Guide

This guide supports three audiences: instructors building a course around *Governed Agentic Systems*, professionals running an internal training program, and self-studying readers who want to check their own understanding. It contains four resources: guidance on course formats and assessment; model-answer sketches for every chapter's review questions; per-part guidance on the exercises; and a rubric for the Part VIII capstone. The answer sketches are deliberately terse. They name the points a correct answer must contain; they are not essays, and for questions that call for design judgment they state evaluation criteria rather than a single right answer. All references to Nornyx behavior in this guide follow the book's claim discipline: they describe the repository at the pinned snapshot, and nothing here extends what the chapters themselves establish.

## 1. How to teach from this book

**The 14-week graduate course.** The book was structured for a two-chapters-per-week pace with three natural checkpoints. A workable schedule: weeks 1–2, Part I (Chapters 1–4), establishing the problem and the vocabulary; weeks 3–5, Part II (Chapters 5–10), the conceptual core — identity, zones, policy semantics, composition, approvals, enforcement; weeks 6–7, Part III (Chapters 11–15), evidence and assurance, ending with the eight questions and the five-test rule fully internalized (a midterm sits well here, built from Part I–III review questions); weeks 8–9, Part IV (Chapters 16–21), the first hands-on weeks with the `nornyx` toolchain; week 10, Part V (Chapters 22–25, with 26–27 as directed reading); weeks 11–12, Part VI (Chapters 28–33), where the three engineering case-study chapters (30–32) support strong seminar discussion; week 13, Part VII (Chapters 34–38), with Chapter 38 assigned as mandatory reading before any capstone claims are written; week 14, Part VIII (Chapters 39–41) and capstone presentations. The capstone project itself should be issued around week 8 so that students build it incrementally as Parts IV–VI supply the machinery.

**The 5-day professional intensive.** Day 1: Chapters 1–4 in the morning, Chapters 5–7 and 10 in the afternoon (Chapters 8–9 as evening reading). Day 2: Chapters 11–15, then Chapters 16–18 with the first toolchain lab (generate, lock, tamper, observe). Day 3: Chapters 19–25, centered on the adapter labs and the bypass demonstration. Day 4: Chapters 28–33, with Chapter 29's CI lane built live and Chapters 26–27 as reference material. Day 5: Chapters 34, 36, and 38 in the morning; a compressed capstone in the afternoon — participants produce a claim register and one failure-injection transcript rather than the full build. Chapters 35 and 37 serve intensives best as take-away reading.

**Self-study.** Three passes work better than one linear read: first Parts I–III without touching the tooling, to acquire the reasoning frame; then Parts IV–VI at a keyboard, doing every exercise marked as toolchain-based; then Parts VII–VIII, writing a real claim register for a system the reader actually operates. Self-study readers should treat the capstone rubric in Section 4 as a self-assessment checklist.

**Assessing the case-study threads.** The five Northstar threads (Atlas, Forge, Ledger, Gateway, Charter) are cumulative: each chapter that touches a thread advances it, so a student who can only summarize a thread's latest scene has not followed it. Effective thread assessments ask for reconstruction across chapters — for example, "trace the Forge stale-approval scene from Chapter 9's binding semantics through Chapter 30's decision surface," or "explain which assurance tier each of Thread D's four integration paths supports and why." The capstone is the summative assessment: Chapters 39–41 model exactly the deliverables (design, claim register, failure-injection transcripts, reconstruction) students should produce, and Section 4 of this guide grades them.

**Prerequisites by audience.** All readers need basic software engineering: version control, CI, APIs, YAML/JSON, and enough Python to read (not necessarily write) short listings. Engineers and architects need nothing further. Security engineers will move faster through Chapters 6, 14, and 34 but should not skip Chapters 7–9, where the policy and approval semantics live. Governance, risk, and audit professionals can treat the code listings as evidence exhibits rather than programs — Chapters 3, 9, 11–13, 35, and 36 are fully accessible without executing anything — but should still watch the toolchain labs performed. No reader is assumed to know agent frameworks, policy engines, or assurance theory; the book introduces all three from first principles.

## 2. Review-question guidance, by chapter

### Chapter 1 — From Deterministic Software to Agentic Behavior

1. The four inherited expectations are specification, testing, review, and least privilege; the four agentic properties are non-reproducible decision paths, a shared forgeable channel for instructions and data, a reachable action set exceeding the authorized set, and harm emerging from sequences of acceptable actions. A full answer pairs each expectation with the property that most undermines it (specification/testing with non-reproducibility, review with the forgeable in-band instruction channel, least privilege with reachable-versus-authorized, per-action testing with emergent sequences) and defends the pairing.
2. The reachable set is fixed by integration (tools, credentials, network, filesystem); the authorized set is fixed by intent. Process-level least privilege bounds only reachability — it cannot express which reachable actions are authorized under which conditions.
3. They have gained a sample: evidence that the advisory context plus this evaluation set produced acceptable behavior on the sampled trajectories. They have not gained a control — nothing deterministic evaluates or enforces the new paragraphs, nothing records decisions, and untested trajectories remain unconstrained.
4. Per-action checks see one action without sequence context; a harmful sequence is composed of individually allowable actions. A sequence-aware control needs history or provenance — what preceded this action and what taint the inputs carry — which is why later chapters propagate taint and bind occurrences.
5. The best answers classify it as luck (or minimization achieved accidentally): no component evaluated an authorization decision, and the reachable set happened not to include publishing. Credit any classification that distinguishes "no enforcement occurred" from "enforcement succeeded."
6. Any pairing works if the asserted control is a documented intention with no evaluator or record (a wiki rule, a prompt) and the demonstrable one produces a mechanical verdict and an artifact (a branch-protection refusal, a CI gate log).

### Chapter 2 — The Governance Gap

1. Architectural because five locally rational artifacts had no shared identity, versioning, or precedence, so no process could detect their disagreement; the missing property is single-sourcing — a shared, versioned representation from which the others derive — which would have made the contradiction computable.
2. Policy drift is divergence among statements of intent; configuration drift is divergence between the governing artifacts and what is actually deployed — for example, a platform setting changed by hand while every document stayed untouched. The remedy differs: reconciliation of representations versus comparison of deployed state against the derived artifact.
3. Framework-adapter drift is dangerous because it is silent at the enforcement boundary: the framework's surfaces change underneath the integration while every governance artifact still reads correctly. The loudness mechanism is exact version pinning enforced at import (or an equivalent compatibility gate), converting the mismatch into a failure at load time.
4. The objection must use the four axes — layer, determinism, evidence, and accountability binding: a probabilistic output filter operates at the wrong layer, yields no deterministic verdict, produces no bound durable record, and binds no accountable decider. The axis most likely unconsidered is accountability binding (accept determinism or evidence with justification).
5. Transferring: functional decomposition into named functions with defined interfaces, and separation of the deciding plane from the acting plane. Not transferring: deterministic payload semantics (agent behavior is probabilistic; media is not model inference) and mandatory, standardized enforcement (no standards regime compels agent frameworks to traverse the control plane). Each non-transfer matters because it bounds what the analogy licenses a designer to assume.

### Chapter 3 — What Governance Can and Cannot Guarantee

1. The sentence collapses at least three layers: validation is a check on evidence-binding conformance, not an observation of behavior; the evidence is supplied by producers whose trust is assumed; and "behaved correctly" is an assurance claim. A defensible restatement: "the supplied evidence stream conforms to its contract and binds to the stated policy revision; on covered surfaces, recorded decisions were consistent with policy."
2. Any signed, hashed record that omits events works: high integrity (digest), high authenticity (signature), low completeness. No cryptography improves completeness because no property of a record establishes what is absent from it — completeness is bought with architectural position (an observer the subject cannot avoid), not computation.
3. The signature buys authenticity (attribution of each record to a producer) and strengthens integrity; it does not buy truth or completeness — a dishonest or selective producer signs happily. The organizational benefit Section 3.3 names is attribution: disputes narrow to a named producer's honesty and accountability attaches.
4. Grade on honest application: a credible "no bypass path" answer requires the control to occupy a position the subject cannot avoid (mandatory interception); for any cooperative or in-process control, the student must produce the direct-call or uncovered-surface bypass.
5. Fail-open means the control's absence is silent and afterwards indistinguishable from correct operation, so an outage becomes an unbounded, unrecorded authorization window — a security incident. Fail-open is right where the decision only observes or reports (or where consequence is trivial and availability paramount); the student must name a genuinely low-consequence decision.
6. A claim with no stated residual asserts that nothing remains unproven, which is almost never true; the empty residual signals either that the analysis was not done or that the claimant is inflating. Stated residuals define the claim's boundary and make it testable.

### Chapter 4 — Core Concepts and Vocabulary

1. Policy-as-code makes rules machine-evaluable but cannot address configuration drift or framework-adapter drift (either is acceptable), because those live between artifacts and deployment; governance-as-code covers the full artifact set — identities, capabilities, approvals, evidence obligations — with versioned, derived artifacts that drift checks can compare.
2. The second team supports the stronger claim about prevention on the covered path — enforcement strength is a property of position, and an unavoidable PEP with a crude PDP still compels; the first team's claim is limited to "when consulted, decisions are high-quality," scoped to the roughly half of paths that consult it.
3. An executable contract requires precise terms, an evaluation procedure, a failure behavior, and an evidence obligation. Without defined failure behavior, Chapter 3's question 6 ("what happens when the enforcing component fails?") has no answer, so the guarantee is conditional on availability in an unspecified way — it is not a contract, merely a behavior.
4. A mesh can enforce that traffic between services traverses it regardless of the workload's cooperation (mandatory interception); it cannot see agent-level semantics — capability, approval state, provenance, sequence position — which the in-process wrapper can express. Implication: compose models; neither placement alone suffices.
5. Same instrumentation because evidence and observability should derive from one truth about what happened; different retention because evidence retention follows obligation class (audit horizons) while telemetry retention follows operational cost — and privacy constraints bind them differently.
6. Tier 1: the claim holds against an adversary who must get a change past design-time validation and review (declared artifacts). Tier 2: it holds against a subject that cooperates — accident, not an adversary who can edit code or call around the wrapper. Tier 3: it holds even against a governed system attempting bypass, because enforcement and evidence are independent of it.

### Chapter 5 — Identity, Capabilities, and Authority

1. Three failures drawn from distinct fields of Section 5.2, for example: no validity interval, so a long-retired agent's string still authorizes; no revocation state, so removal is deletion and past decisions become uninterpretable; no framework binding, so nothing connects the role string to the runtime construct actually acting; no status, so decommissioned and active are indistinguishable. Any three distinct-field failures count.
2. Revocation must be a recorded state, not deletion, because past decisions were made against that identity; if deleted, the audit question "who was this actor, and was it valid at decision time?" becomes unanswerable.
3. Capability is a type (a bounded action surface), permission is a binding of capability to identity, authority is the per-situation computed conclusion from identity, permission, scope, obligations, and time. Credit examples showing each pairwise conflation's defect: treating capability as permission grants by existence; treating permission as authority ignores conditions and expiry; storing authority invites reuse outside its situation.
4. Shorter tool lists reduce count, not scope. A complete answer rescopes across the four dimensions of Section 5.5 (actions, resources/scope, conditions such as time and situation, and obligations), for example narrowing each remaining tool's resource scope and validity rather than merely dropping five.
5. The attack: attacker influences task creation → creates a task whose stated requirements include the high-risk capability → handoff confers whatever the task requires → receiving agent now holds the capability without any delegation record, attenuation, or bound. The step-by-step form matters; the lesson is that a handoff supplying missing authority is an escalation primitive.
6. The tool decides on its own ambient credential, not the caller's designated authority — a confused deputy. The single change: make the caller's identity, capability, and scope travel with the call and have the decision use them (attenuated per-caller credentials acceptable). It does not fix the tool's own compromise, injection into the query itself, or the reach of the underlying credential.

### Chapter 6 — Trust Zones and Boundaries

1. Any example distinguishing reachability from information-class boundaries works: two services on one segment where one handles regulated data (same segment, different zones); replicas of one logical component split across data centers (different segments, one zone). The point: network boundaries constrain packets; zones constrain authority and information flow, declared rather than wired.
2. Membership as a separate record makes capabilities zone-relative and gives the binding its own lifecycle: it can be revoked independently of the identity, and it carries its own validity interval. Two enabled actions: eject an identity from a zone without destroying it; expire or review a membership on its own schedule (also acceptable: scrutinize multiple membership as a declared bridge).
3. Absence from an allowlist is an omission — a default that a later editor can "fix" by adding the category, with no signal that a decision was reversed; never-share is a recorded prohibition whose removal is a visible, reviewable act. The realistic process: incremental allowlist growth under delivery pressure quietly legitimizes a flow nobody decided to permit.
4. As a pipeline property: retrieved content may inform generated text but must not alter what the agent is permitted to do — no permission-affecting field is derived from retrieval. Exploiting a violating system needs only content that reaches the context window (a document, a page), which is easy precisely because retrieval is designed to ingest the untrusted world.
5. The classifier reduces the incidence of successful persuasion (a useful filter); it cannot detect all injections, and it leaves the guarantee structure unchanged — untrusted content still reaches a position of authority when it slips through, so consequences are unbounded by the classifier. The structural answer bounds consequences via capability and flow constraints regardless of detection.
6. Egress most needed on `treasury-data` (its never-share categories — `account_credentials`, `full_pan` — mean leakage is the consequence that leaves the zone); ingress most needed on `payment-exec` (the consequence is unauthorized work entering the execution zone; the case-study canon gates it on ingress). Credit answers arguing from the direction that carries the consequence.

### Chapter 7 — Policy Semantics and Deterministic Evaluation

1. Grade on correct identification of the four elements — subjects, actions, conditions, closed decision domain — in the chosen prose statement, and on a rewrite that makes all four explicit (plus obligations where the statement implies them). Most prose policies omit the decision domain entirely.
2. Deny-plus-out-of-band loses the connection between the decision and the approval it names: the PDP can no longer state which approval, by whom, with which bindings, would convert the outcome, and the enforcement point cannot re-check an approval it never hears about. The failure: actions proceed on out-of-band say-so with no bound record, or stall with no path to legitimate execution.
3. The argument: under default-deny, a request is allowed only if some permission matches and no prohibition matches; adding a prohibition or a non-matching rule can only preserve or remove allows; only a new matching permission with no overriding prohibition can create one. This monotonicity is what lets layers compose without a lower layer silently weakening a superior one.
4. Three problems: determinism is lost (the directory's answer varies over time, so identical requests yield different decisions); testability is lost (tests now require a live directory in a known state); audit reproducibility is lost (re-evaluating a past decision needs the directory's state at that instant, which nobody kept). The fix is explicit inputs.
5. A safety property can be violated by a finite prefix ("never send category X externally"); "every case is eventually resolved" (or similar liveness/average-behavior requirements) is not. The monitor's conservative approximation converts it, for example a deadline ("resolved within N days or escalate"), and the answer must state the legitimate behavior blocked (slow-but-legitimate work refused or escalated).
6. Substituting the current time makes the decision depend on when evaluation happened to run: an expired approval passes because the pipeline ran late, or replaying a past decision yields a different verdict. The incident is intermittent, environment-dependent, and invisible in the policy text — exactly the hard-to-diagnose class. Failing on a malformed instant keeps evaluation total and deterministic over explicit inputs.

### Chapter 8 — Policy Composition, Provenance, and Inheritance

1. The stricter approval expiry is a narrowing — monotone, and the engine accepts it, with provenance recording Treasury as the source. The looser token budget is an attempted override (widening) of a superior control and must be refused or forced through an explicit exception record; a compliant engine never resolves it silently by precedence.
2. Last-writer-wins makes effective meaning depend on write order rather than on the rule algebra, violating monotonicity: a later write can silently widen a superior constraint. Acceptable for preferences where any consistent value is fine; unacceptable where a weakening must be impossible or loud.
3. Over-aggressive for languages where order, duplication, or interaction between rules is semantically significant (order-dependent resolution, counted or rate-based rules, scoped parameters). A real change hidden: reordering rules in a first-match-wins language changes which rule fires while the normalized set is unchanged.
4. Needed artifacts: the composed effective policy for that date with per-element provenance (showing the rule and its source layer/version); the lock binding the reviewed sources and derived artifacts; the deployment's recorded revision/digest tying what ran to the locked set; plus evidence that the check was enforced (CI/lock verification). Each contributes one link: rule present, provenance of it, reviewed-equals-deployed, and enforcement of the comparison.
5. False drift: previously identical documents now canonicalize differently, so checks report drift where nothing changed. Identity collapse: two identifiers that differed only in Unicode form now collapse, merging semantically distinct rules. Migration: version the canonicalizer, recompute identities under both versions during a transition, and treat the upgrade as a compatibility event with re-review, not a silent library bump.
6. A timestamp makes lock bytes vary across identical inputs, destroying reproducibility: two honest regenerations differ, so byte comparison can no longer distinguish "changed content" from "changed clock," and drift loses its meaningful zero.

### Chapter 9 — Approvals, Exceptions, and Human Accountability

1. Two mechanisms refuse: exact revision binding (the executed manifest's digest no longer matches the approved one) and invalidation (`manifest_change` occurred). Expiry does not — 17 hours is inside the window. The more useful diagnostic is defensible either way if argued: invalidation names *what happened in the world* (the manifest changed), while the revision mismatch names *which binding broke* at the moment of use; credit answers that connect diagnostic usefulness to what the operator must do next.
2. The circularity argument is accountability-free: a gate exists to inject information from outside the system being gated; if the system (or another instance of it) supplies the approval, the gate returns no information — its pass rate is a property of the same distribution it was meant to check. That holds even where nobody cares about legal responsibility.
3. Violated: maker–checker extended past the agent to the human who directed it — the instructing engineer is a maker. Commit metadata shows the agent as author, so it cannot catch this; the rule needs the recorded task initiator: "the identity that initiated the agent's work is ineligible to approve the resulting change."
4. Two structurally different explanations: (a) the gate covers genuinely low-risk, well-prepared requests, so fast approval is appropriate; (b) approval fatigue — volume has converted review into rubber-stamping. A distinguishing measurement: relate decision time to request complexity or seed the queue with deliberately defective requests and measure the catch rate (also acceptable: rejection-rate-versus-volume trends).
5. Checking grant last means every binding (scope, role, actor type, evidence, revision, expiry, invalidation, revocation) has been verified first, so a failure code names the binding that actually broke. Checked first, a non-granting or wrongly-bound record short-circuits to an uninformative "not granted," hiding forged or misbound records and destroying the diagnostic value of the refusal.
6. Some controls must be enumerated as never-exceptable, and "AI systems may not approve" is one: an exception to it would recreate the circularity of Question 2 regardless of owner, expiry, or compensating controls. A well-designed system refuses structurally rather than routing to a human who might grant it — the deny is intrinsic, not discretionary.

### Chapter 10 — Enforcement Models and Fail-Closed Design

1. Questions from this chapter: where does enforcement sit in the action path — which surfaces must traverse it and which can avoid it? Is enforcement mandatory or cooperative? What happens when the decision service fails? What evidence is produced, by whom? What identity can the enforcement point establish? A policy language answers none of the placement questions — bypassability, failure behavior, and coverage are properties of topology, not of the language.
2. Strongest to weakest for "Atlas cannot publish externally": sandbox and IAM boundary (reachability removed or the resource itself refuses — no cooperation needed), then gateway/egress proxy and mesh (mandatory for traffic that must transit them, modulo alternate paths), then library interception, then in-process wrapper (fully cooperative — a direct call defeats it). Credit orderings that justify placements by the mandatory-versus-bypassable dimension.
3. Reconstructable: that the outage occurred and its window. Not reconstructable: which actions ran unevaluated during the window, what would have been denied — nothing was decided or recorded, and afterwards the window is indistinguishable from normal operation. The one change: record every action taken on the fail-open path with a degraded-mode label, so the window's contents are at least enumerable.
4. Weakened property: revocation/decision freshness — a denial issued now takes effect up to fifteen minutes late, so the guarantee becomes "no unauthorized action, except within a 15-minute staleness bound after a policy or approval change." Unacceptable for action classes where a single stale allow is irreversible — payment submission, external disclosure, production deployment.
5. The gateway's record is independent of the governed process (harder to suppress, but semantically thin: endpoints, verbs, identities); the wrapper's record is semantically rich (capability, policy revision, decision basis) but produced inside the bypassable process. An audit wants both: the independent record corroborates that the rich record is not fabricated or incomplete on that path.
6. The limit: controlling signaling does not control media — an agreement in the control plane binds nothing unless the action's path traverses an element that applies it. Predicted gap: any tool call or network effect that does not transit the enforcement element (direct SDK calls, side channels) proceeds ungoverned however perfect the decisions are.

### Chapter 11 — Runtime Evidence as an Engineering Artifact

1. Using Table 11.1's dimensions: it still fails on binding (log lines do not bind to subject identity and policy revision, so they cannot support a claim about a governed decision) and on completeness over a declared scope (verbose logs are still best-effort emission with no completeness contract; sampling/loss is acceptable for diagnosis, fatal for claims). Verbosity raises volume, not claim-support.
2. Supplied evidence is produced by (or via) the governed system and validated only for conformance; observed evidence is produced by an observer the subject cannot influence. For an in-process adapter, a conforming stream cannot rule out omission (events never emitted) or fabrication (events describing actions that did not happen as recorded) — both invisible from inside because validation checks internal consistency, not correspondence to the world.
3. The misleading case: the validation semantics or checks changed between then and now, so "pass" under the old validator does not mean what the reader assumes under the current one; the missing field is the validator version recorded in the report.
4. The design: store the digest of the summary content in the evidence, not the content itself — the auditor verifies the reviewed artifact by recomputing its digest against the original store. It stops working when the original is deleted or mutated (nothing left to hash against), or when the auditor needs the content itself, not just its identity.
5. Executing an external scanner requires the governance layer to hold execution privileges and to trust the scanner's runtime behavior, widening its own attack and failure surface; a compromise or misbehavior of the scanner now occurs inside the trust domain of the layer whose records everyone else trusts. Import keeps the layer's required privileges minimal: it validates and binds artifacts, it does not run foreign code.

### Chapter 12 — Integrity, Replay, Ordering, and Determinism

1. The single claim: these bytes are those bytes (content identity). The five unsupported claims and mechanisms: truth — an independent observer; producer identity — signatures/attestation; completeness — an unavoidable observer (architectural position); authorization of the change — approval records and review; honesty of the producer — trust established outside the record (attestation, organizational controls). Accept any reasonable mechanism per claim consistent with Chapter 3.
2. A single lock adds arity and co-binding: one check covers the *set* — source, revision, schemas, and all artifacts together — so a consistent substitution of several files at once (regenerate artifact A from modified source while leaving B) cannot pass, and reviewers get one digest to review instead of six independent ones with no statement that they belong together.
3. The error: ordering two producers' events by timestamp assumes synchronized clocks establish happened-before, which they do not. Minimum change: declared causal references in the records (an event carries the identifier of the event or artifact it depends on), so cross-stream order is grounded in the data, not the clocks.
4. The sequence number is transport identity — a legitimate rewrite/resend renumbers, and including it would let a replayer evade detection by renumbering. Excluding the timestamp is safe only when occurrence identity distinguishes repeated *work* (new occurrence) from repeated *records* (same occurrence, same substance); without occurrence identity, two legitimate identical jobs at different times would collide as replays.
5. (a) Duplicate/replay — same occurrence and attempt with substantively identical content under a different transport identity. (b) Invalid — a successful occurrence cannot be retried; attempt 2 after a success violates the rule. (c) Legitimate repeated work — a new occurrence is the sanctioned way to repeat. (d) Invalid — attempts must be contiguous; the missing attempt 2 is a visible gap.
6. The timestamp makes every generation differ, so the drift gate reports change on every regeneration; the lock can never verify twice. Two weeks later the organization has either learned to ignore the gate (drift blindness — the real failure) or wrapped it in exceptions; the gate has become noise precisely when a genuine tamper would need it.

### Chapter 13 — Assurance Tiers

1. The three mechanisms map: a declaration/validated contract is Tier 1, defeated by running something never declared; a cooperative wrapper is Tier 2, defeated by not calling it (direct invocation); an independent enforcement point (gateway/sandbox/IAM) is Tier 3, defeated only by a change to the environment itself — reconfiguring or compromising the enforcement component or finding a path that does not traverse it.
2. A total bypass never invokes the decision point, so nothing records a request, a denial, or anything else — the stream is simply shorter. Implication: absence of denial records is not evidence of absence of action; reasoning from a cooperative stream's silence is unsound.
3. Declaring a producer and referencing a signature are affordances for accepting external evidence — they do not make the evidence independent. Tier 3 requires the full list: authenticated producer identity, verified attestation, capture protected from the governed system, deployment binding, independent logging, and demonstrated coverage.
4. Draft-only replies: consequence is bounded by the human sender (harm requires human action), adversary is accident/injection, so Tier 2 on the drafting surfaces (with Tier 1 substrate) is defensible. Automatic sending changes consequence to direct external effect, which demands independent enforcement — Tier 3 — on the sending surface. Grade the method (consequence, adversary, tier), not the exact conclusion.
5. Grade the rewrite on structure: named surfaces ("for tool invocations on the wrapped `X` surface at revision R…"), named mechanism and tier ("cooperative enforcement, Tier 2, declared surfaces only"), evidence scoped ("supplied evidence validating against schema S"), and an explicit "not claimed" clause (bypass paths, completeness, unwrapped surfaces).
6. Tier 3 buys independence and loses semantic expressiveness and generality: properties over rich governance vocabulary — approval bindings, delegation bounds, provenance obligations, relationships among declared records — are easy to express and check at Tier 1 over artifacts but hard for a network-level enforcement point to even see. Tiers name what defeats a claim, not overall quality.

### Chapter 14 — Bypass, Coverage, and Negative Controls

1. Two states cannot distinguish "the authors declined to wrap this and you must avoid or externally control it" (unsupported — obligation on the deployer to manage) from "this surface is the integrator's to wrap" (unwrapped — obligation on the integrator to build and test). The async surface is the first, graph construction the second; a single "uncovered" label loses who owes what.
2. Strongest attacker-aid argument: the list is a menu of unguarded doors. Counterarguments from Section 14.3: a capable attacker can derive the gaps from the code anyway; defenders and deployers cannot compensate for gaps they do not know; and the published list is what makes the positive claim falsifiable rather than vacuous. For a sceptical security officer, the falsifiability argument (or attacker-derivability) is usually most persuasive — credit the justification.
3. The test provides: continuous verification (it runs on every change, so the statement stays true or the build fails) and precision (it pins the exact behavior — the direct call executes ungoverned — rather than a paraphrase that can drift from reality). A README paragraph is neither checked nor exact.
4. "Denied" requires asserting the decision and its code, the recorded denial evidence, and the absence of side effects; "raised an exception" asserts only that something threw. Minimum assertions: decision code equals the expected denial; the denial event pair exists in the stream; the side-effect ledger is unchanged. Each catches a defect: wrong-reason denials, enforcement without recording, and exception-after-effect respectively.
5. With an exact pin: import fails loudly; the inventory and claim remain true for the tested version; deployment is blocked until the adapter is retested and the pin widened — the upgrade becomes a governance event. With a range: the new entry point loads silently, the inventory is now incomplete, the claim "tool invocations are governed" is silently false, and the deployment continues.
6. A precise rewrite must carry surface ("access to production databases via the wrapped query tool"), mechanism ("is denied by the cooperative in-process decision point"), conditions ("when calls traverse the wrapper, at contract revision R"), and tier ("a Tier 2, cooperative claim; direct connections are out of scope"). Grade on all four elements being present and each qualification word identified.

### Chapter 15 — Testing Governance Claims

1. The rewrite must bind surface (the specific egress/call paths), mechanism (which component evaluates and blocks), and condition (coverage and revision). The five obliged tests: allow (authorized flow proceeds and is recorded first), deny (unauthorized send refused, with code, no effect), failure (decision path broken → action does not proceed), bypass (direct path behavior pinned as declared), evidence (records validate and bind). Falsifiers: for each, the concrete observation that would break the claim (e.g., data sent despite denial; action proceeding during injected outage).
2. Recording after the action permits a window where the action ran with no decision on record; a crash or kill in that window yields an effect with no evidence. The defect that passes a non-ordered allow test: a wrapper that executes first and back-fills the decision record — both artifacts eventually exist, but the claim "decided before acted" is false.
3. A conformance suite is written against the contract (not one implementation), is portable across implementations, and defines what any implementation must do including failure behavior. For a governance interface this matters more because multiple adapters/integrations claim the same guarantees, and the suite is what makes "wrapped" mean the same thing across them.
4. Scenario: a request arrives with a malformed decision instant after the relevant approval has expired; the fallback substitutes the current clock — or conversely, replays under a corrected clock — so an evaluation that should have failed (or denied on expiry) instead allows. The evidence then misrepresents the evaluation instant: the recorded decision looks properly time-bound when the time was invented by the evaluator.
5. Precisely: for any request, the composed policy of layers A and B never allows what A alone denies — composition can narrow, never widen; the asymmetry is that added denials and requirements take effect while added permissions cannot defeat superior prohibitions. A violating scheme that reviews clean: per-layer files each look fine, but the merge resolves conflicts by precedence or last-writer-wins, so the lower file's permission overrides the upper file's denial only at composition time.
6. The conditional bypass test asserts what the *platform configuration currently permits*, so it detects configuration regression: an administrator relaxing branch protection changes the test's expected outcome and the suite says so. A fixed assertion would keep passing against a weakened platform. The organizational event it catches: a well-meaning settings change (or admin exception) that silently invalidates the standing bypass claim.
5. Honest two-sentence form: "Forge's declared capabilities exclude secrets access, and on evaluated paths the cooperative decision point denies it — a Tier 2, declared-surfaces claim. Whether any path to secrets exists outside those surfaces is carried by the platform's secret-store access controls and credential scoping, not by the governance layer." Grade on both sentences naming their enforcement.
6. Desirable because refusals are precisely where later scrutiny concentrates — a refused merge yields the request, the denial, its code, and the presented-and-rejected approval, so the audit of the contested case is pre-written. It defends against the Chapter 9 failure mode of approval decisions leaving no interrogable record (stale/invalid approvals silently retried or disputed with no evidence of what was presented when).

### Chapter 31 — Multi-Agent Governance

1. Different deployments is a statement about infrastructure, not authority — nothing prevents one identity (or one delegation chain) from holding both capabilities across those deployments. The correct phrasing is over identities and capabilities, at all times, across all zones, including delegation: "no identity may hold or acquire, directly or by delegation, both `payment.draft` and `payment.approve`."
2. Refusals: the delegation's `onward_delegation: denied` stance; the depth bound (`max_depth: 1` already consumed); and attenuation failures if the second hop's actions or scope are not a subset of what the analyst received (plus expiry if applicable). Reporting all reasons matters because fixing only the first reported violation and resubmitting would serially discover the rest — and because a review needs to see every independent rule the design relies on.
3. The declaration layer can declare distinct capabilities per band with distinct approval requirements — the escalation structure; it cannot compute which band a given case's amount falls into, because band selection happens at runtime in the application. The structural alternative: encode bands as separate capabilities and put the selecting component in the trusted computing base — acknowledging the trust relocated to whatever maps amounts to capabilities.
4. Three consistent situations: nothing deny-worthy was attempted; denials occurred on surfaces outside cooperative coverage (never evaluated, so never recorded); or the producer omitted denial events. Validation distinguishes none of them — it establishes conformance of the supplied records, and absence of records is not evidence of absence of action.
5. Auditor's reason: if handoffs conferred authority, reconstructing "by what authority did this agent act?" would require replaying the task-assignment history rather than reading delegation records — the authority trail dissolves into workflow history. Attacker's reason: task creation becomes an escalation primitive — craft a task whose execution requires the capability you want conferred (Chapter 5's handoff attack).
6. A mandatory gateway on one step makes that step's claim independent of the agents' cooperation — Tier 3 for that surface. Tiers attach to surfaces, not systems: the workflow's other steps remain cooperatively covered or uncovered, so the workflow-level claim is still bounded by its weakest consequential surface.

### Chapter 32 — Enterprise Governance Hierarchies

1. The rule: a lower layer may narrow a superior control and may never widen it silently — weakening must be a visible, bounded, owned exception. "Silently" is the implementable part: a checker cannot always decide what a widening *means* organizationally, but it can force any widening through an explicit record and refuse the silent path — visibility is computable even where legitimacy is a human question.
2. The implemented check compares normalized rule *sets* for equality, so Treasury's added rule reads as drift (`DRIFT`), and `--write` would remove it, restoring the canonical set. Correct for the mechanism as designed — it maintains "member matches canonical" — but wrong for the five-level model, where a stricter Treasury rule is a legitimate narrowing that the equality check cannot distinguish from a weakening.
3. Roaming: a visited network may restrict a subscription and never grant an entitlement the home subscription lacks — likewise, a permissive execution environment can only fail to enforce; it cannot *create* authority, which comes from declared policy. Two non-transfers (any two): mandatory standardized enforcement, the single authoritative subscriber store, deterministic signaling semantics, or the standards regime among operators.
4. The third defect was invisible because the author's environment/context silently satisfied an assumption the control did not state — a scope or observable that held on the author's machine only; only a cold-start trial by someone without that context exposed it. The practice: fresh-environment runs by a person (or pipeline) without the author's setup — the same discipline as the installed-wheel smoke.
5. Parameters make the widening/narrowing question a semantic comparison of patterns rather than set inclusion: the classifier must decide whether one path pattern denies a superset of another, which ranges from expensive to undecidable depending on the pattern language. Trade-off: expressiveness for authors against analyzability of the four-outcome classification — every added rule feature must be checked against the classifier it may break.
6. Failure modes and their careless fail-open answers: unreachable source — proceed with the last cached copy (or none); version skew — take whichever is newest; authentication failure — fall back to unauthenticated fetch; partial/corrupt content — use what parsed. Each careless answer silently weakens; the fail-closed answers refuse and surface diagnostics — which is why the repository's `ref` is offline, local-only, and fail-closed, and cross-repository references are a declared non-goal.

### Chapter 33 — Operations, Observability, and Incident Response

1. Regenerating would have overwritten the discrepancy — the evidence of *what* diverged and therefore of what someone changed and why — replacing an investigable red state with an uninvestigable green one. The green gate would then be worse than the red: the control fired correctly, and the operator's "fix" would have destroyed the finding while recording that all is well. First runbook rule: do not restore green.
2. Lock-check failure in the payments lane: page — an integrity signal on a high-consequence lane. Denial rate doubling for one capability: trend/ticket — behavioral, needs investigation not wake-up. Median approval decision time falling to four seconds: ticket/periodic review — a fatigue indicator (Chapter 9's arithmetic), not an outage. Workspace drift on a business-unit repo: ticket — integrity-adjacent but design-time, blocking merges rather than runtime authority. Grade the routing logic (integrity pages, behavior trends).
3. Lost properties: binding (spans do not carry contract digest, lock digest, and subject revision per record), completeness over a declared scope (tracing samples and drops by design), and validated identity semantics (occurrence/attempt rules, ordering and replay checks — plus retention by obligation class). Legitimate remaining role: detection and operational monitoring — telemetry detects, evidence reconstructs.
4. The three candidates: producer crash or loss before write (events never persisted), deliberate deletion/tampering in the store, or a producer that skipped emission (omission). Distinguishers: process and infrastructure logs for the crash window; store access/immutability audit for tampering; producer-side instrumentation or corroborating records for omission. Renumbering destroys the gap itself — the one artifact all three explanations are explanations *of* — making the stream internally consistent and the question unaskable.
5. The two-question test (does the path decide authority, or only record/report?): drift gate — decides what may merge: fail closed (block the lane). Evidence recorder — recording is the point; keep recording through everything, and if the recorder itself fails on a governed path, that path's actions must not proceed unevidenced (fail closed on the governed action). Dashboard — observation only: keep serving/degrade open. Approval check on a gated capability — decides authority: fail closed.
6. The record must precede the action because its existence *before* consequence is what distinguishes an authorized, bounded deviation from a retroactive rationalization; supporting evidence can legitimately take time to assemble. Reversed, the claim "this action was taken under an authorized exception" becomes unmakeable — any record created after the fact is indistinguishable from a cover story.

### Chapter 34 — Threat Modeling Governed Agentic Systems

1. The coverage matrix is what scopes every downstream claim: change an entry from unsupported to wrapped (or delete an unsupported row) and no test fails, no code changes, and nothing happens immediately — but risk assessments, claim registers, and audits now inherit a false scope. The deferred consequence: a bypass on the misdescribed surface is later treated as impossible, controls are not funded there, and the eventual incident is both unprevented and unexplained.
2. Grade the mapping, not one canonical answer: e.g., the malicious contract author reaches contracts/composed policy (unhelpful mitigation: runtime interception); the compromised runtime reaches evidence streams (unhelpful: parser hardening); the hostile tool package reaches the capability/declaration layer (unhelpful: log retention); the prompt-injecting source reaches decisions via context (unhelpful: lock verification); the over-permissive operator reaches approvals/exceptions (unhelpful: cryptography generally); the dishonest evidence producer reaches the record (unhelpful: stream validation, which it passes). Require one asset class and one genuinely non-helping mitigation per attacker.
3. Duplicate keys: two readers (or the reviewer and the evaluator) can see different values for the same key — the reviewed document and the enforced document diverge, a silent weakening primitive. Implicit coercion: values like unquoted `no`/`on` silently change type and meaning between contexts. Rejecting is correct because any resolution rule preserves a document with two defensible readings; a governance loader must ensure the enforced document *is* the reviewed one, so ambiguity is an error.
4. Expect the three residuals consistent with the chapter's boundaries: omission by a producer (an internally consistent short stream validates — relied-on control: producer trust and organizational controls, owned by the application/producer team); lock regeneration by a writer with repository access (relied-on control: branch protection, signing, and human review of digest diffs, owned by the platform); and unauthenticated approver identity (relied-on control: the identity provider and access to the approval channel, owned by the identity/IAM function). Grade on precision plus named external owner.
5. Route one: conventional supply-chain execution — install hooks and lifecycle scripts running code — addressed by software-composition analysis and the scanner's hook/script detection. Route two: the package *declares capabilities* and self-describes — no conventional analogue; a governance layer that takes declarations as authority lets the package grant itself permission by writing a file, which is what the claim-versus-evidence discipline (untrusted claims cross-checked against observed content) addresses.
6. Grade the walk: the four steps (roughly: risk assessment consumes the sentence; the control is rated effective; compensating investment is withdrawn or never made; the uncovered path is exercised and the loss lands unattributed). The strong answer identifies the earliest step where an honest qualifier ("on evaluated paths," "declared surfaces only") would have kept the residual visible and funded, and prices the qualification honestly — usually a harder conversation, not more engineering.

### Chapter 35 — Mapping Controls to Standards

1. The two elided layers are the interpretation (what the clause is taken to require in this system's terms) and the realization status with evidence (whether and how the control is actually in place, with artifacts). Eliding interpretation makes the row uncheckable — no one can say what would falsify it; eliding realization/evidence lets a documented-but-unimplemented or unlabeled-extension control satisfy the clause on paper.
2. Grade the method: the student must describe a plausible outcome failure for the chosen clause and honestly test whether the named realization prevents it (blocks it from occurring), detects it (surfaces it afterward), or neither. "Neither, and here is why the row is still worth keeping" is a legitimate conclusion when defended (e.g., the row scopes a residual and directs an auditor's attention).
3. The tenet: enforcement is imposed by the infrastructure per-request regardless of the subject's cooperation (never trust, always verify — the PEP is unavoidable). The inversion: in cooperative enforcement the subject *chooses* to consult the decision point. The rest transfers faithfully — per-request evaluation, least privilege, explicit policy, identity-centric decisions — because those are properties of decision-making the cooperative layer does implement; only the compulsion tenet inverts.
4. From the four NOT CLAIMED lines: approver identity → an identity provider plus signing/authentication of approvers (external identity system; contributes to Tier 3 evidence); no unwrapped path to the same effect → a mandatory enforcement point covering the surface (Tier 2 → Tier 3); stream completeness → an independent observer producing evidence outside the subject's influence (Tier 3 evidence production); legal sufficiency → never claimable by tier transition at all — it requires legal/regulatory determination outside engineering.
5. Compliance-washing raises perceived assurance without changing the control (qualifiers eroded through summarization); checkbox mapping associates *some* control with a clause without mechanism-outcome fit. Re-deriving a row from its artifact detects compliance-washing (the artifact will not support the inflated words); checkbox mapping can survive re-derivation — the artifact exists and matches the row; only the inversion test (would it prevent or detect the outcome's failure?) exposes it.
6. For generating: generated mappings cannot drift from the artifacts and stay pinned to a revision. Against: interpretation is a human argument — a generator can emit what controls exist, but not what a clause means for this system or whether mechanism fits outcome; generating that would fake the layer that makes rows checkable. The defensible split: generate the artifact inventory, digests, revisions, and realization-status evidence; keep interpretation, caveats, and fit judgments hand-written.

### Chapter 36 — Audit and Evidence Packages

1. The rewrite must have all four properties: a named decision class (e.g., external shares of research summaries), an exact subject revision, a fixed time interval, and decomposition into clauses that artifacts discharge (was an approval required and present; did it bind to the revision and actor; was the decision recorded before the share; does the shared payload match the reviewed one). Each clause must be paired with its discharging artifact: zone/approval declarations, composed effective policy, lock, approval record, event stream, payload digest.
2. Grade per step: step 2 (composition) — e.g., the composed effective governance cannot be reproduced or a pack hash mismatches (`PACK_LOCK_MISMATCH`); step 3 (lock) — a field-level `AN_LOCK_*` failure; step 5 (events) — strict validation failure (gap, replay, ordering). For each, the surviving findings are those not resting on the broken step: the strong answer traces each of the conclusion's findings to its supporting steps and downgrades exactly the dependent ones to "cannot be established," not all of them.
3. Two errors: regeneration answers a question about *today's* contract, not the reviewed revision the decision was made under (temporal substitution); and it destroys the independence of the check — comparing a regenerated artifact with itself verifies the generator, not the preserved record. The audit must run against the preserved lock and artifacts.
4. The change: an independent observer/enforcement point on the sharing path that the workload cannot avoid, producing evidence outside Atlas's influence — establishing the completeness claim at Tier 3 for that surface. The inherited assumption: trust moves to the new producer — the observer's own integrity, coverage, and deployment binding become the premises.
5. Strongest deletion argument: failed packages invite misreading — a future reviewer may take a failed validation as the system's current state, and duplicate packages complicate discovery. Defeat: a failed package is evidence — of what was found, when, and that remediation was a new run against a new revision (Section 36.5's rule: repaired streams are fabrications); and Chapter 16's release-history lesson shows a preserved, itemized failure record is what makes an organization's statements checkable — deleting it converts verifiable history into assertion.
6. Dashboard screenshot: counterfeits the events/interpretation steps (an unbound rendering with no revision, digest, or validator) — exposed by the worksheet columns demanding the artifact and its binding. Unversioned export: counterfeits the contract/lock steps (nothing ties records to a reviewed revision) — exposed by the binding-tuple column. Conclusion outrunning producers: counterfeits the interpretation step — exposed by the "what this does not establish" column, which the overclaiming sentence would have to contradict.

### Chapter 37 — Enterprise Adoption and Platform Strategy

1. Grade the method: correct stage assignment justified by exit criteria met and unmet, the stage's forbidden claim restated in local vocabulary, and a real (or realistic) internal sentence that violates it. The most common finding is a Stage 2–3 workflow described in Stage 5–6 language ("our agents are fully governed"), which is precisely the inflation the ladder's forbidden claims exist to catch.
2. Per-workflow scoring follows from Chapter 13's rule that tiers attach to surfaces and evidence packages, never to whole systems: an organization is a portfolio of workflows at different stages, and a single organizational score would be either the minimum (uninformative) or an average (an inflation instrument). The maturity claim, like the tier claim, must name its denominator.
3. One-click approval optimizes throughput and dwell time — the metrics of fatigue, not of control; it destroys the informed-approver property (the attestation no longer corresponds to anything considered) while preserving the audit trail's appearance. Structurally correct interventions: raise thresholds so fewer actions gate, expire and re-scope standing approvals, monitor time-to-approve as a control-health signal, and reclassify low-consequence actions out of the gate with named compensating controls.
4. The language must be neutral because policy outlives any platform and must compose across them; enforcement is necessarily specific because compulsion is a property of a particular platform's topology. Costs: encoding policy in a platform dialect couples the organization's authority model to a vendor and makes multi-platform claims unverifiable; building a "neutral" enforcement layer means rebuilding mandatory interception the platforms already provide — expensive, and usually weaker than the native boundary.
5. Honest Tier 2 answers: Q3 (evidence) — "supplied evidence produced by our in-process adapter, validated for conformance; not independently observed"; Q5 (bypass) — "direct calls under the wrapper bypass enforcement; here is the coverage inventory and the bypass test"; Q8 (not claimed) — "completeness, producer independence, and uncovered surfaces." Inflated versions replace supplied with verified/tamper-proof, delete the bypass admission or call it mitigated, and shrink the not-claimed list to boilerplate. Grade on marking the exact changed words.
6. Counterparts: profiles → domain profiles/reference policy postures constraining options to interoperable subsets; conformance regimes → conformance suites and adapter conformance testing against pinned versions; staged migration → the maturity ladder with interworking between governed and ungoverned estates. The break in each: telecom conformance certifies deterministic protocol behavior, while agent behavior is probabilistic — a passing governance conformance suite certifies the *mechanisms*, never the agent's conduct.

### Chapter 38 — Limitations, Open Problems, and Research Directions

1. Grade on the triple per row: the limitation, its introducing chapter, where the repository itself states it (validator-report limitation text, non-goals, coverage inventories, packaging metadata), and the compensating purchase — e.g., supplied-not-observed (Ch. 11/20; validator limitations text; compensate with an independent observer); cooperative-and-bypassable (Ch. 10/13; coverage inventory and bypass test; compensate with a mandatory enforcement point); locks-bind-bytes-not-producers (Ch. 12/18; lock docs; compensate with signing and branch protection); no-human-authentication (Ch. 9/19; SPI docs; compensate with an IdP and signed approvals).
2. Deny is the only verb that computes: a require rule compiles to a pending-evidence obligation that only organizational process discharges, so a team can believe controls are enforced when they are promissory notes — the invited failure is unearned confidence across the whole rule set. Substring matching is a *quality* limitation of a mechanism that does run — it invites coverage gaps, which negative testing can find. A weak control that executes is more diagnosable than a control that never executes at all.
3. The argument: agents that create tools mint new capabilities at runtime; declaration-time governance cannot enumerate them, so the only containment that scales is an enforcement boundary the self-created tool cannot avoid — which is exactly problem 2, portable mandatory enforcement without platform lock-in. Until enforcement is portable and unbypassable, self-created tools are governed only by cooperative wrapping they have no reason to traverse.
4. Schneider's boundary: execution monitors can enforce only safety properties — violations witnessed by a finite prefix. Classifications: never-share tokens across the partner boundary — enforceable (safety), on evaluated paths; every mission eventually produces an evidence package — liveness, not enforceable; approximable by a deadline, otherwise audit-only; no two same-reviewer approvals per quarter for the same requester — enforceable in principle as a safety property over histories *if* the monitor holds complete approval history; with supplied, possibly incomplete records it degrades to audit-only. Credit reasoned prefix-decidability arguments.
5. A mechanical defect breaks one mechanism; leaving it unacknowledged falsifies the book's framing claim — that the discipline's value rests on honest claim boundaries — which is the load-bearing wager. Chapter 16's changelog lesson is the same test applied to the repository: confidence attaches to a project's *statements* being checkable and corrected, so an unacknowledged defect damages the statements, which is more than the mechanism.
6. The reply must use only Section 38.5's supported claims: the boundary's purchases are specific and demonstrated — an explicit authorized set, computable change visibility, reconstructable decisions on covered surfaces, and honest claim boundaries — and the "unproven" concession concerns the economic wager and open research problems, not those mechanisms. Stating limits is what makes the claims auditable; the vendor's inference ("limits admitted, therefore skip governance") would equally condemn every control discipline that states residual risk, and the alternative on offer — documentation plus optimism — states none.

### Chapter 39 — Capstone: Designing the Complete Northstar System

1. `ref` is an authoring-time mechanism: contracts consume the canonical policy at generation, fail-closed, so members cannot drift *by construction* at the point of generation; `workspace-check` is an independent verification across repositories that detects divergence however it arose (including edits after generation, or members not using `ref`). Only `ref`: post-generation divergence and non-referencing members go undetected. Only `workspace-check`: drift is detected but constantly re-introduced, since nothing makes authoring consume the canonical source.
2. Declaring `payment.submit` unheld makes the prohibition a *checkable absence*: the engine can evaluate and deny requests against a declared capability, the denial is recorded, and the inventory's "no identity holds it" is a reviewable statement. Undeclared, a request for it fails as an unknown reference — indistinguishable from a typo — and no artifact states the design decision that submission is reserved to systems outside the agent estate.
3. The pre-existing component is the platform's branch protection: independent in Chapter 13's sense because it is operated by the platform, sits in the merge path, and the agent cannot bypass or influence it. The Tier 1 remainder: the correspondence between the contract's declared gate (roles, maker–checker, bindings) and the platform's actual configuration — a declaration-level claim, with the residual that platform administrators can alter settings (independence is organizational).
4. The composed `human_approval` module fixes role authority — approvals name roles, and naming an individual fails the module's structural checks (the non-human/role-authority diagnostics). This is a feature: role-based approval survives personnel change and keeps the contract free of personal data; the person-to-role mapping lives in the identity infrastructure (the IdP and the approval channel's access control), where it belongs.
5. The two words: "evaluated paths" (accept "on evaluated paths"). Without them the claim asserts the categories are *never* shared by any route — a whole-system, mandatory-enforcement claim that the cooperative Tier 2 mechanism cannot support; the rewrite commits Chapter 13's tier inflation (attaching a claim to the system rather than to named surfaces, and using mandatory verbs for cooperative control).
6. Grade the application: the student removes every not-yet-existing component (gateways, hierarchy engines, planned observers) from a real design and tests each claim sentence for survival. Strong answers find at least one claim that quietly depended on roadmap machinery and restate it one tier lower or with a residual — the same discipline the capstone applies to its own register.

### Chapter 40 — Capstone: Implementation, Verification, and Assurance Review

1. Demonstrating the insufficient gate first shows *why* the full-artifact gate exists: a diff confined to one artifact passes any gate that compares only a subset of files, so the injection proves the failure mode before proving the remedy. It re-enacts the historical defect in the toolchain's own guidance: a hand-written artifact inventory that missed the file that mattered — the reason the full gate takes its file list from the generation manifest.
2. If actor type were checked first, a stale approval presented by a non-human would produce `APPROVAL_NON_HUMAN`, and an auditor could wrongly conclude that a non-human attempted to approve *the current revision* — i.e., that the record was otherwise applicable and the only defect was the actor. Revision-first ordering ensures the diagnostic names the binding that actually broke: the record was never about this subject state at all.
3. Identity-resolution failure is an attribution failure, not a policy decision: no identity was established, so there is no subject for a denial event to be *about*, and fabricating one would put invented identity into evidence (Chapter 24's rule against substituted defaults). The correct behavior is a loud fail-closed error on the configuration path; "record everything" would make the stream's identity claims false precisely where identity is in doubt.
4. In explicit mode, occurrence and attempt identity distinguish repeated work, so the timestamp is transport — including it would let a replayer evade the fingerprint by restamping. To repeat identical work legitimately, a producer must open a new occurrence (or a new attempt after a recorded failure): the identity fields, not the clock, are what sanction repetition.
5. F6 exercises the coverage-inventory register fields: the surface's `unsupported` status, the claim that the path fails without executing or recording, and the residual assigned to the deployer. The same transcript falsifies the register if the call *succeeds* (or executes before failing) — an unsupported surface that runs is a false inventory entry, and the register's honesty, not just the adapter, is what failed.
6. Paraphrase typically drops the qualifiers that scope the report — "conformance of supplied records," "declared surfaces," validator version and limitations — converting "the stream validates" into "the behavior was correct": the conclusion-outruns-producers inflation (supplied read as observed, pass read as truth). Verbatim quotation keeps the proof boundary attached to the finding.

### Chapter 41 — Lessons, Trade-offs, and Future Architecture

1. One artifact per judgment, e.g.: explicit authority — the consolidated identity/capability inventory (meaningless if authority stays implicit); proportional control — the tier-selection table walking consequence per action class; honest coverage — the coverage inventory and the register's residual column; fail-closed defaults — the failure-injection transcripts showing gates blocking (F-series); evidence before claims — the claim register itself, every row bound to mechanisms and transcripts. Accept any correct artifact-judgment pairing with the dependence argued.
2. The argument: a bypassed control produces the *appearance* of governance — green gates, an audit trail, confident claims — so risk assessment consumes the appearance and compensating controls are never built; an absent control at least leaves the gap visible. Counterexample: a control that is bypassed on one path but genuinely covers others still prevents real harm — better than nothing *if* its coverage is honestly stated. The reconciliation: bypassed-and-honestly-scoped beats absent; bypassed-and-claimed-total is worse than absent.
3. Section 41.3's test: does consequence outlive undo, and does trust span parties? An internal single-user research agent fails both — adopt the vocabulary (declared capabilities, a claim register, honest scoping) but not the machinery. The two arrival triggers: the agent's actions acquire external or irreversible consequence (customer data, production systems, external publication), and a second party arrives who must trust the first (customers, auditors, regulators, or simply more teams).
4. Hazard: two independently authored policies diverge — the split-brain/semantic-drift problem (Chapters 19 and 26): the gateway enforces something other than what was reviewed. Compiling from the reviewed contracts keeps one authoritative source; the containing gate is digest binding of the projection to its source revision plus a drift gate/conformance replay verifying the deployed projection still corresponds.
5. Best-supported answer: authenticated producers (identity-bound, attested evidence) — it deletes the residual-dependency cells that say "supplied, not observed," "producer honesty assumed," and "approver identity not authenticated" across the register's evidence-dependent rows. Accept a defended case for the projection compiler (deleting semantic-drift and unbypassable-enforcement residuals on gateway-backed rows) — grade on correctly matching the component to the residual cells it would remove.

## 3. Exercise guidance, by part

**Part I (Chapters 1–4).** All exercises are paper designs: inventories, classifications, decompositions, and rewrites; none requires the `nornyx` CLI, and most benefit from being run against a system the student actually knows. Common pitfalls: enumerating the reachable set at the level of "tools" rather than credentials, filesystem scope, and network capability (Chapter 1); writing remediation plans when the exercise asks for diagnosis (Chapter 2); and decomposing claims into restatements rather than genuinely narrower claims with distinct evidence producers (Chapter 3). A strong submission is concrete and falsifiable: named artifacts, named components, and explicit "I could not determine X" entries. Instructors should grade the honesty of the gaps as heavily as the completeness of the inventories — the Part I exercises exist to teach students that most environments cannot answer these questions, and a submission with no gaps usually means the student stopped looking.

**Part II (Chapters 5–10).** Predominantly paper: identity records, delegation designs, zone models, decision tables, approval-record and fatigue analyses. Two exercises need tooling: Chapter 8's workspace exercise requires the pinned `nornyx` CLI (`workspace-check`, `--write`), and Chapter 10's wrapper exercise requires Python (any test framework; no Nornyx dependency). Pitfalls: designing delegations without all four bounds (attenuation, depth, expiry, scope); zone models whose never-share lists are empty or decorative; and Chapter 10 wrappers whose tests assert exceptions rather than absence of effects. A strong submission states, for every design, the validation errors it must produce when edited carelessly — the mark of a student who has internalized that declarations are worth only what checks them. Fatigue-budget arithmetic (Chapter 9) should show its numbers.

**Part III (Chapters 11–15).** Mixed. Paper: evidence contracts, fingerprint designs, tier maps, claim registers, coverage inventories, claim rewrites. Toolchain: Chapter 11's evidence-import probe and Chapter 12's deterministic-generation/lock-tamper experiment require the pinned CLI; Chapter 15's failure-injection exercise needs a codebase the student can break, and its five-test sorting exercise needs an existing test suite. Pitfalls: treating the five tests per surface as five degrees of thoroughness rather than five distinct falsifiers; tier maps that assign tiers to systems instead of surfaces; and claim-register entries with empty "not claimed" lists. Strong submissions quote observed diagnostic codes and exit codes exactly (never paraphrased) and, in the failure-injection table, flag every row where observed behavior diverges from the control's description — divergences found are the point, not a defect of the submission.

**Part IV (Chapters 16–21).** The toolchain part: nearly every exercise requires the pinned Nornyx release installed locally (contract authoring with `check` and `policy-check`, generation, profiles and network locks, tamper-and-verify cycles, drift gates, forbidden-content scans). Exercises are offline and local by design; no network or external services are needed. Pitfalls: running against an unpinned Nornyx version and then being confused by diagnostic differences; editing generated artifacts instead of contracts and misreading the resulting drift; forgetting `--as-of` and misattributing validation failures; and reading `require` rules as enforcement when recording deliberate-breakage results. A strong submission is a lab notebook: exact commands, exact codes, exit statuses, and a short interpretation for each observation distinguishing what the tool proved from what it merely reported. The deliberate-breakage tables (Chapters 17–18) should classify every failure as caught-as-error, caught-as-warning, or not caught.

**Part V (Chapters 22–27).** The most environment-heavy part. Chapters 23–24 exercises need Python with the adapter distribution and the exactly pinned framework versions (CrewAI, LangGraph) — instructors should pre-build an environment, because the exact pins are themselves a lesson that costs class time. Chapter 22's evaluation matrix, Chapter 24's stream-prediction, Chapter 25's obligations design, and Chapter 26's projection specification and outage runbook are paper. Chapter 27's package scan needs the CLI and a real third-party bundle (scanned, never installed or executed — enforce this rule explicitly). Pitfalls: testing denials by exception rather than by ledger/side-effect absence; claiming coverage the inventory does not support; and writing projection specs that quietly compile away the untranslatable fields. Strong submissions attach the coverage claim each artifact supports, in Chapter 14's qualified form, to every deliverable.

**Part VI (Chapters 28–33).** Requires the CLI plus a CI environment the student can modify (any runner; the repository's reference pipeline is the model). Chapters 28–29 build authoring and pipeline lanes: fixtures asserting decision codes, three-diff review instrumentation, drift and lock gates routed by the exit-code contract. Chapters 30–32 are mostly paper against real organizational material — decision tables, gate write-ups in the eight-element format, platform-settings verification, workspace and conflict-classifier designs; Chapter 31's delegation-breaking and Chapter 32's two-level workspace need the CLI. Chapter 33 mixes signal instrumentation with evidence-preservation labwork. Pitfalls: pipelines that treat exit 1 and exit 2 identically; gate write-ups whose negative-test and bypass elements are left empty; exercising `--write` without predicting what sync will delete. Strong submissions run their own negative tests and report what the bypass attempt actually found, including "nothing, and here is why that is suspicious."

**Part VII (Chapters 34–38).** Almost entirely paper, aimed at the student's real environment: threat-tree extensions, mitigation registers, standards-mapping rebuilds, audit-chain walks, maturity and cost-ledger assessments, limitation tables. Chapter 34's tree extension and Chapter 38's falsification attempt are the exceptions needing the repository at the pinned snapshot. Pitfalls: registers with empty residual-risk cells or unowned rows; mapping rows rebuilt clause-first instead of control-first; audit worksheets that quietly fill gaps instead of reporting empty rows; and falsification write-ups that soften a reproduction into a "note." A strong submission names owners and dates for everything it hands to another team, reports ratios and findings rather than volume, and — for Chapter 38 — treats a failed falsification attempt as a verified claim with the write-up as its evidence artifact, exactly as the exercise instructs.

**Part VIII (Chapters 39–41).** The capstone part: exercises require the full local toolchain (contracts, workspace, generation, locks, evidence validation) and, for the adapter-wired injections, the Part V environment; the register, design-extension, and proposal exercises are paper. Chapter 39's exercises extend the design (new register rows, a fourth application, a cross-thread seam); Chapter 40's re-run the failure-injection programme and reconstruction; Chapter 41's produce cost ledgers and the Tier 3 proposal. Pitfalls: extending the design by modifying existing contracts when the exercise forbids it; injections reported without the fourfold deliverable (boundary, diagnostic family, preserved evidence, lesson); reconstructions that quietly consult the builder — every out-of-band question is a missing binding and must be reported as one. A strong submission's register rows are auditable sentences: scope, producer, tier, residual, each testable by a stranger. Grade against Section 4's rubric.

## 4. Capstone assessment criteria

The Part VIII capstone asks students to produce what Chapters 39–41 model: an integrated design for the Northstar estate (or an equivalent system of the student's own), a claim register, a working build with failure-injection transcripts, and an audit reconstruction. The rubric below grades seven dimensions. Weight them roughly equally, with one governing rule stated first because it overrides local scoring.

**The overclaiming rule.** The book's standard, established in Chapter 34 and enforced throughout Part VIII, is that an inflated claim is a vulnerability, not a style problem: broad unsupported claims lose credit *even when the accompanying code works*. A capstone whose build is flawless but whose register says "the executor cannot submit payments" without the "on evaluated paths" qualifier has failed the discipline the capstone exists to assess. Deduct for every claim that exceeds its mechanism, its coverage, or its tier, regardless of build quality; do not restore the credit because a demonstration succeeded. Conversely, an honestly scoped claim with a stated residual never loses credit for the residual's existence — only for its concealment.

**1. Explicit authority.** *Excellent:* a consolidated inventory of identities, capabilities, zones, and memberships in which every authority-bearing relation is a declared record with bounds and intervals, and deliberate absences (unheld capabilities, unapprovable identities) are declared and checkable rather than implicit. *Adequate:* the inventory covers all agents and capabilities but relations (delegations, memberships) are partly implicit, or absences are asserted in prose rather than declared. *Inadequate:* authority must be inferred from code, tool wiring, or narrative; the reachable and authorized sets are not distinguishable from the submission.

**2. Proportional control.** *Excellent:* tier and gate selection walked per action class from consequence and adversary, with low-consequence surfaces deliberately left lightly governed and the reasoning shown; no gate exists that the consequence analysis does not justify. *Adequate:* high-consequence surfaces are correctly gated but the analysis is implicit or uniform — everything gets the same treatment regardless of consequence. *Inadequate:* control placement is decorative (heavy gates on trivial actions, none on irreversible ones) or tier selection ignores the adversary actually in scope.

**3. Claim–evidence traceability.** *Excellent:* every register row names its enforcing mechanism, its evidence artifacts (by digest or path), and its supporting tests or transcripts; a reader can traverse from any claim sentence to the artifact that supports it without asking the author. *Adequate:* rows name mechanisms and evidence classes but some links are generic ("CI logs") rather than specific artifacts. *Inadequate:* claims are supported by narrative, demonstration recollection, or the build's general existence.

**4. Honest coverage and status labeling.** *Excellent:* wrapped, unsupported, and unwrapped surfaces are inventoried with reasons; every design element is correctly labeled **[implemented]**, **[guidance]**, or **[extension]**; the design passes the deletion test — remove every extension and every remaining claim stays true. *Adequate:* the inventory exists but a few obligations are misassigned (unsupported versus unwrapped), or one extension quietly props up a current-tense claim. *Inadequate:* coverage is asserted per system rather than per surface, or roadmap machinery is presented as deployed.

**5. Fail-closed behavior.** *Excellent:* failure injection attacks the design's own boundaries (drift, lock tamper, stale approval, replay, identity duplication, artifact removal, and the deliberate bypass), each with the fourfold deliverable — boundary, diagnostic family, preserved evidence, lesson — and the bypass that succeeds is *predicted* by the register. *Adequate:* injections run but deliverables are partial (diagnostics quoted without preserved evidence), or a failure mode surprises the register without the register being corrected. *Inadequate:* only happy-path demonstration; or a gate that fails open is discovered and left undiscussed.

**6. Audit reconstructability.** *Excellent:* a consequential decision is reconstructed through the full chain — contract, composition, lock, approval, events, artifacts, interpretation — from preserved artifacts alone, with the conclusion carrying its scope block; an assessor (or a colleague, per the Chapter 40 exercise) can repeat it without out-of-band questions. *Adequate:* the chain is traversable but requires the author's help at one or two steps, and those gaps are acknowledged. *Inadequate:* reconstruction depends on regenerated artifacts, dashboards, or memory; or the conclusion outruns its worksheet.

**7. Cost and limitation awareness.** *Excellent:* the submission carries a cost ledger (authoring effort, pin maintenance, approval latency, expertise) and a limitations statement in Chapter 38's form — each limitation paired with the compensating control someone must supply — plus a defensible judgment about where the machinery is *not* warranted. *Adequate:* costs and limits are listed but not connected to obligations or decisions. *Inadequate:* the submission presents governance as free, or its limitations section is boilerplate that names no transferred obligation.

A borderline submission is best resolved by the register: read only the claim register and its residual column, then check three rows at random against the build. If the rows survive, the student has learned the discipline; if they inflate, no quantity of working code compensates.

## 5. Recommended learning paths

**Software engineer new to governance.** Read linearly through Part I, then 5, 7, 9, 10 from Part II; 11, 13, 14, 15 from Part III; all of Part IV at a keyboard; then 22–25, 28–30. Rationale: engineers profit most from reaching the toolchain quickly, but Chapters 13–15 must precede it or the tooling reads as features rather than claims. Return for 6, 8, 12, and Part VII before attempting the capstone.

**Enterprise architect.** 1–4, 6, 8, 10, 13, 16, 18, 26, 31–33, 37, 39, 41. Rationale: the architect's questions are placement, composition, and hierarchy — enforcement models, composition and locks, external enforcement points, the Charter thread, and adoption strategy — with the capstone design chapter as the worked template. Chapters 22–25 can be skimmed for the coverage vocabulary.

**Security engineer.** 3, 5, 6, 10, 12, 13, 14, 15, then 19–21, 23 (for the bypass test), 26, 27, 34, and 38. Rationale: the discipline's security content concentrates in authority confusion, bypass and coverage, negative controls, threat modeling, and the parser/lock hardening of Chapter 34; Chapters 7–9 should still be read before writing any policy, since approval semantics are where security reviews of these systems most often go wrong.

**Governance, risk, or audit professional.** 1–4, 9, 11, 13, 14, 16 (for status labels), 18 (for locks, lightly), 28, 33, 35, 36, 37, 39–40 (register and reconstruction sections). Rationale: this path builds the ability to read claims, evidence packages, coverage inventories, and registers critically without operating the toolchain; Chapter 36 is the professional core, and Chapter 35's seven-field row structure transfers directly to existing mapping work.

**Platform or SRE.** 4, 10, 12, 13, 15, 16, 18, 19, 21, 22, 25, 26, 29, 33, 37. Rationale: this role owns enforcement placement, pipelines, determinism, diagnostics-as-interface, exit-code routing, and the operational health of the governance layer itself; Chapters 29 and 33 are the center of gravity, with Chapter 26 framing the platform enforcement points the role already operates.

**Researcher or graduate student.** The full linear read is the intended path; where time is short, prioritize 3, 7, 12, 13, 14, 20, 24, 31, 34, 38, with 38's open problems and research directions as the bridge to the literature. Chapter 15's property-based framing and Chapter 12's identity hierarchy repay close reading.

**Policy author.** 6, 7, 8, 9, 17, 18, 28, 32, then 14 and 35. Rationale: this path runs from policy semantics through composition and approvals into the concrete authoring workflow and the hierarchy thread; Chapter 28 is the daily-work chapter, and Chapters 14 and 35 teach the author to write rules and mappings whose claims survive review. Chapter 38's account of the rule language's limits is mandatory before writing production contracts.
