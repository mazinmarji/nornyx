# Instructor and Self-Study Guide

This guide supports three audiences: instructors building a course around *Governed Agentic Systems*, professionals running an internal training program, and self-studying readers who want to check their own understanding. It contains four resources: guidance on course formats and assessment; model-answer sketches for every chapter's review questions; per-part guidance on the exercises; and a rubric for the Part VIII capstone. The answer sketches are deliberately terse: they name the points a correct answer must contain, and for questions that call for design judgment they state evaluation criteria rather than a single right answer. All references to Nornyx behavior follow the book's claim discipline — they describe the repository at the pinned snapshot, and nothing here extends what the chapters themselves establish.

## 1. How to teach from this book

**The 14-week graduate course.** The book was structured for a two-chapters-per-week pace with three natural checkpoints. A workable schedule: weeks 1–2, Part I (Chapters 1–4), establishing the problem and the vocabulary; weeks 3–5, Part II (Chapters 5–10), the conceptual core — identity, zones, policy semantics, composition, approvals, enforcement; weeks 6–7, Part III (Chapters 11–15), evidence and assurance, ending with the eight questions and the five-test rule fully internalized (a midterm sits well here, built from Part I–III review questions); weeks 8–9, Part IV (Chapters 16–21), the first hands-on weeks with the `nornyx` toolchain; week 10, Part V (Chapters 22–25, with 26–27 as directed reading); weeks 11–12, Part VI (Chapters 28–33), where the three engineering case-study chapters (30–32) support strong seminar discussion; week 13, Part VII (Chapters 34–38), with Chapter 38 assigned as mandatory reading before any capstone claims are written; week 14, Part VIII (Chapters 39–41) and capstone presentations. The capstone project itself should be issued around week 8 so that students build it incrementally as Parts IV–VI supply the machinery.

**The 5-day professional intensive.** Day 1: Chapters 1–4 in the morning, Chapters 5–7 and 10 in the afternoon (Chapters 8–9 as evening reading). Day 2: Chapters 11–15, then Chapters 16–18 with the first toolchain lab (generate, lock, tamper, observe). Day 3: Chapters 19–25, centered on the adapter labs and the bypass demonstration. Day 4: Chapters 28–33, with Chapter 29's CI lane built live and Chapters 26–27 as reference material. Day 5: Chapters 34, 36, and 38 in the morning; a compressed capstone in the afternoon — participants produce a claim register and one failure-injection transcript rather than the full build. Chapters 35 and 37 serve intensives best as take-away reading.

**Self-study.** Three passes work better than one linear read: first Parts I–III without touching the tooling, to acquire the reasoning frame; then Parts IV–VI at a keyboard, doing every exercise marked as toolchain-based; then Parts VII–VIII, writing a real claim register for a system the reader actually operates. Self-study readers should treat the capstone rubric in Section 4 as a self-assessment checklist.

**Assessing the case-study threads.** The five Northstar threads (Atlas, Forge, Ledger, Gateway, Charter) are cumulative: each chapter that touches a thread advances it, so a student who can only summarize a thread's latest scene has not followed it. Effective thread assessments ask for reconstruction across chapters — for example, "trace the Forge stale-approval scene from Chapter 9's binding semantics through Chapter 30's decision surface," or "explain which assurance tier each of Thread D's four integration paths supports and why." The capstone is the summative assessment: Chapters 39–41 model exactly the deliverables (design, claim register, failure-injection transcripts, reconstruction) students should produce, and Section 4 of this guide grades them.

**Prerequisites by audience.** All readers need basic software engineering: version control, CI, APIs, YAML/JSON, and enough Python to read (not necessarily write) short listings. Engineers and architects need nothing further. Security engineers will move faster through Chapters 6, 14, and 34 but should not skip Chapters 7–9, where the policy and approval semantics live. Governance, risk, and audit professionals can treat the code listings as evidence exhibits rather than programs — Chapters 3, 9, 11–13, 35, and 36 are fully accessible without executing anything — but should still watch the toolchain labs performed. No reader is assumed to know agent frameworks, policy engines, or assurance theory; the book introduces all three from first principles.

## 2. Review-question guidance, by chapter

### Chapter 1 — From Deterministic Software to Agentic Behavior

1. The four expectations: specification, testing, review, least privilege. The four properties: non-reproducible decision paths, one forgeable channel for instructions and data, reachable set exceeding authorized set, harm from sequences. Credit defensible pairings (least privilege ↔ reachable-versus-authorized; review ↔ in-band instructions; testing/specification ↔ non-reproducibility and sequences).
2. Reachable is set by integration (tools, credentials, network, filesystem); authorized is set by intent. Process-level least privilege bounds only reachability and cannot express conditions of authorization.
3. Gained: a sample — evidence that this advisory context passed these trajectories. Not gained: a control — nothing deterministic evaluates, enforces, or records the new paragraphs on untested trajectories.
4. Per-action checks see one action without history; a harmful sequence is composed of individually acceptable actions. A sequence-aware control needs history or provenance — what preceded the action, what taint its inputs carry.
5. Best classified as luck (or accidental minimization): no component evaluated an authorization decision. Credit any answer distinguishing "no enforcement occurred" from "enforcement succeeded."
6. Any pairing works where the asserted control is a documented intention with no evaluator or record (wiki rule, prompt) and the demonstrable one produces a mechanical verdict plus an artifact (branch-protection refusal, CI gate log).

### Chapter 2 — The Governance Gap

1. Architectural: five locally rational artifacts had no shared identity, versioning, or precedence, so no process could detect disagreement. The missing property is single-sourcing — a shared versioned representation from which the others derive.
2. Policy drift is divergence among statements of intent; configuration drift is divergence between artifacts and deployed state — e.g., a platform setting changed by hand with no document touched. Remedies differ: reconcile representations versus compare deployment against the derived artifact.
3. It is silent at the enforcement boundary: framework surfaces change beneath the integration while every artifact still reads correctly. The loudness mechanism: exact version pinning enforced at import, failing at load time.
4. The objection uses the four axes: a probabilistic output filter sits at the wrong layer, yields no deterministic verdict, produces no bound durable record, binds no accountable decider. Most likely unconsidered: accountability binding (determinism or evidence acceptable with justification).
5. Transferring: functional decomposition with defined interfaces; separation of deciding plane from acting plane. Not transferring: deterministic payload semantics (agent behavior is probabilistic) and mandatory standardized enforcement (no standards regime compels frameworks to traverse the control plane) — each bounds what a designer may assume.

### Chapter 3 — What Governance Can and Cannot Guarantee

1. Confusions: validation checks evidence-binding conformance, not behavior; the evidence is supplied by trusted-by-assumption producers; "behaved correctly" is an assurance claim. Defensible restatement: "the supplied stream conforms to its contract and binds to the stated revision; recorded decisions on covered surfaces were policy-consistent."
2. Any signed, hashed record that omits events: high integrity and authenticity, low completeness. No cryptography helps because no property of a record establishes what is absent — completeness is bought with architectural position, not computation.
3. Buys authenticity (attribution) and strengthens integrity; not truth or completeness — a dishonest or selective producer signs happily. The organizational benefit: attribution narrows disputes to a named producer's accountability.
4. Grade the application: a credible "no bypass" answer requires a position the subject cannot avoid; any cooperative control must yield the direct-call or uncovered-surface bypass.
5. Fail-open makes the control's absence silent and afterwards indistinguishable from correct operation — an unbounded unrecorded authorization window. Fail-open is right where the decision only observes or reports, or consequence is trivial and availability paramount.
6. An empty residual asserts nothing remains unproven — almost never true; it signals missing analysis or inflation. Stated residuals define the claim's boundary and make it testable.

### Chapter 4 — Core Concepts and Vocabulary

1. Policy-as-code cannot address configuration or framework-adapter drift (either), which live between artifacts and deployment; governance-as-code covers identities, capabilities, approvals, and evidence obligations with versioned derived artifacts drift checks can compare.
2. The second team — enforcement strength is a property of position, and an unavoidable PEP compels even with a crude PDP. The first team's claim is only "when consulted, decisions are good," scoped to the consulting half of paths.
3. An executable contract needs precise terms, evaluation procedure, failure behavior, evidence obligation. Without failure behavior, Chapter 3's question 6 has no answer: the guarantee is conditional on availability in an unspecified way.
4. A mesh enforces traversal regardless of workload cooperation (mandatory interception); it cannot see capability, approval state, provenance, or sequence position, which the wrapper expresses. Implication: compose models.
5. Same instrumentation for one truth about what happened; different retention because evidence follows obligation class and audit horizon, telemetry follows operational cost — and privacy binds them differently.
6. Tier 1: holds against an adversary who must get a change past design-time validation and review. Tier 2: holds against accident — a cooperating subject, not one who can edit code or call around the wrapper. Tier 3: holds even against the governed system attempting bypass.

### Chapter 5 — Identity, Capabilities, and Authority

1. Three failures from distinct Section 5.2 fields, e.g.: no validity interval (retired agent's string still authorizes); no revocation state (removal is deletion, past decisions uninterpretable); no framework binding (nothing links the string to the runtime construct); no status (decommissioned and active indistinguishable).
2. Past decisions were made against that identity; if deleted, "who was this actor, and was it valid at decision time?" becomes unanswerable. Revocation is a recorded state, not deletion.
3. Capability is a type, permission a binding, authority a per-situation computed conclusion. Credit examples showing each conflation's defect: capability-as-permission grants by existence; permission-as-authority ignores conditions and expiry; stored authority invites reuse outside its situation.
4. Fewer tools reduces count, not scope. Rescope across the four dimensions — actions, resources, conditions, obligations — narrowing each remaining tool's scope and validity.
5. Steps: attacker influences task creation → crafts a task requiring the high-risk capability → handoff confers what the task requires → receiver holds it with no delegation record or bound. A handoff supplying missing authority is an escalation primitive.
6. The tool decides on its own ambient credential — confused deputy. Fix: make the caller's identity, capability, and scope travel with the call and base the decision on them. Unfixed: the tool's own compromise, injection in the query, the credential's reach.

### Chapter 6 — Trust Zones and Boundaries

1. Any pair distinguishing reachability from information-class boundaries (shared segment, different data classes; split segments, one logical zone). Network boundaries constrain packets; zones constrain authority and information flow, and are declared, not wired.
2. Separate membership makes capabilities zone-relative with an independent lifecycle. Enabled actions: revoke a membership without destroying the identity; expire or review it on its own schedule (or: scrutinize multiple membership as a declared bridge).
3. Allowlist absence is an omission a later editor can "fix" silently; never-share is a recorded prohibition whose removal is a visible act. The process: incremental allowlist growth under delivery pressure legitimizes a flow nobody decided.
4. Pipeline property: retrieved content may inform generated text but never alters permissions — no permission-affecting field derives from retrieval. Exploitation needs only content reaching the context window, easy because retrieval ingests the untrusted world by design.
5. The classifier reduces successful persuasion; it cannot detect all injections, and the guarantee structure is unchanged — content that slips through still reaches authority. Capability and flow constraints bound consequences regardless of detection.
6. Egress on `treasury-data` (its never-share categories mean leakage is the outbound consequence); ingress on `payment-exec` (unauthorized work entering the execution zone is the consequence; the canon gates it on ingress). Credit direction-of-consequence reasoning.

### Chapter 7 — Policy Semantics and Deterministic Evaluation

1. Grade on identifying the four elements — subjects, actions, conditions, closed decision domain — and a rewrite making all four (plus implied obligations) explicit. Prose policies most often omit the decision domain.
2. Deny-plus-out-of-band loses the link between decision and named approval: the PDP cannot state what would convert the outcome, and the enforcement point cannot re-check an approval it never hears about. Failure: actions proceed on unbound say-so, or stall with no legitimate path.
3. Under default-deny, allow requires a matching permission and no matching prohibition; adding rules can only preserve or remove allows unless the addition is an unopposed permission. This monotonicity lets layers compose without silent weakening.
4. Determinism lost (directory answers vary over time); testability lost (tests need a live directory in known state); audit reproducibility lost (re-evaluation needs the directory's past state, which nobody kept).
5. Safety: violable by a finite prefix ("never send category X"). Not: liveness/aggregate requirements ("every case eventually resolved"). Approximation: a deadline with escalation; it blocks slow-but-legitimate work.
6. Substitution makes the decision depend on when evaluation ran: expired approvals pass because a pipeline ran late; replays yield different verdicts. Intermittent, environment-dependent, invisible in policy text. Failing keeps evaluation total and deterministic over explicit inputs.

### Chapter 8 — Policy Composition, Provenance, and Inheritance

1. Stricter expiry: narrowing — accepted, provenance recording Treasury. Looser budget: attempted override (widening) of a superior control — refused or forced through an explicit exception record, never resolved silently by precedence.
2. Last-writer-wins makes meaning depend on write order: a later write can silently widen a superior constraint, violating monotonicity. Fine for preferences; not for safety-relevant policy.
3. Over-aggressive where order, duplication, or rule interaction is semantically significant. Hidden change: reordering in a first-match-wins language changes which rule fires while the normalized set is unchanged.
4. Needed: the composed effective policy for that date with per-element provenance; the lock binding sources and derived artifacts; the deployment's recorded digest; evidence the check was enforced in CI. Contributions: rule present, source of it, reviewed-equals-deployed, comparison enforced.
5. False drift: identical documents now canonicalize differently. Identity collapse: Unicode-variant identifiers merge distinct rules. Migration: version the canonicalizer, compute identities under both during transition, treat the upgrade as a compatibility event with re-review.
6. A timestamp makes identical inputs produce differing bytes: honest regenerations differ, byte comparison can no longer separate "changed content" from "changed clock," and drift loses its meaningful zero.

### Chapter 9 — Approvals, Exceptions, and Human Accountability

1. Refusing: exact revision binding (executed manifest digest no longer matches) and invalidation (`manifest_change`). Expiry does not — 17 hours is inside the window. Either diagnostic is defensible if argued: invalidation names what happened in the world; the mismatch names the binding that broke at use.
2. The argument is accountability-free: a gate injects information from outside the gated system; if the system supplies its own approval, the gate returns no information — its pass rate is a property of the distribution it was meant to check.
3. Violated: maker–checker extended to the human who directed the agent. Commit metadata names the agent as author, so it cannot catch this. Rule: the recorded task initiator is ineligible to approve the resulting change.
4. Two explanations: genuinely low-risk well-prepared requests, or fatigue-driven rubber-stamping. Distinguishing measurement: decision time versus request complexity, or seeded defective requests and the catch rate.
5. Binding checks first make the failure code diagnostic — it names the binding that broke. Grant-first would return an uninformative "not granted" for misbound or forged records, hiding what actually failed.
6. Some controls are enumerated never-exceptable, and this is one: an exception would recreate Question 2's circularity regardless of owner, expiry, or compensating controls. The refusal is structural, not discretionary.

### Chapter 10 — Enforcement Models and Fail-Closed Design

1. Ask: which surfaces must traverse the enforcement point and which can avoid it; mandatory or cooperative; failure behavior; what evidence, by whom; what identity it can establish. A policy language answers none of the placement questions — those are topology, not language.
2. Strongest to weakest: sandbox and IAM boundary (reachability removed or resource refuses), gateway/mesh (mandatory for transiting traffic, modulo alternate paths), library interception, in-process wrapper (a direct call defeats it). Credit rankings justified by mandatory-versus-bypassable.
3. Reconstructable: that the outage occurred and its window. Not: what ran unevaluated, what would have been denied — nothing decided or recorded. The one change: record every action taken on the fail-open path with a degraded-mode label.
4. Weakened: decision freshness — denials and revocations take effect up to fifteen minutes late; the guarantee becomes time-bounded. Unacceptable for irreversible actions: payment submission, external disclosure, production deployment.
5. The gateway record is independent but semantically thin; the wrapper record is rich (capability, revision, basis) but produced inside a bypassable process. An audit needs both: the independent record corroborates the rich one.
6. The limit: controlling signaling does not control media — a decision binds only if the action's path traverses an applying element. Predicted gap: tool calls or network effects that never transit the enforcement element proceed ungoverned.

### Chapter 11 — Runtime Evidence as an Engineering Artifact

1. Still fails on binding (log lines bind to no subject identity or policy revision) and completeness over a declared scope (best-effort emission has no completeness contract). Verbosity raises volume, not claim support.
2. Supplied: produced via the governed system, validated for conformance only. Observed: produced by an observer the subject cannot influence. A conforming adapter stream cannot rule out omission or fabrication — validation checks internal consistency, not correspondence to the world.
3. The validation semantics may have changed between then and now, so "pass" no longer means what the reader assumes. The preventing field: the validator version in the report.
4. Store the digest of the summary, not the content; the auditor recomputes against the original store. Stops working when the original is deleted or mutated, or when the auditor needs the content itself.
5. Execution requires the governance layer to hold execution privileges and trust the scanner's runtime behavior, widening its attack and failure surface inside the trust domain everyone else relies on. Import keeps its required privileges minimal.

### Chapter 12 — Integrity, Replay, Ordering, and Determinism

1. The claim: these bytes are those bytes. The five others: truth — independent observer; producer identity — signatures/attestation; completeness — an unavoidable observer; authorization — approval records and review; honesty — trust established outside the record.
2. Arity and co-binding: one check covers the set — source, revision, schemas, artifacts together — so a consistent multi-file substitution cannot pass, and reviewers get one digest with a statement that the pieces belong together.
3. Timestamp ordering assumes synchronized clocks establish happened-before; they do not. Minimum change: declared causal references in the records, grounding cross-stream order in data.
4. The sequence number is transport identity — a replayer could evade by renumbering, and legitimate resends renumber. Excluding the timestamp is safe only with occurrence identity separating repeated work from repeated records; without it, legitimate identical jobs would collide as replays.
5. (a) Duplicate/replay — same identity, different transport. (b) Invalid — a successful occurrence cannot be retried. (c) Legitimate repeated work — a new occurrence. (d) Invalid — contiguity violated; attempt 2 missing.
6. Every regeneration differs, so the gate fires always and the lock never verifies twice; within weeks the organization ignores the gate or wraps it in exceptions — noise exactly when a real tamper needs it.

### Chapter 13 — Assurance Tiers

1. Declaration/contract — Tier 1, defeated by running something never declared. Cooperative wrapper — Tier 2, defeated by not calling it. Independent enforcement point — Tier 3, defeated only by changing the environment: reconfiguring or compromising the component, or finding a non-traversing path.
2. A total bypass never invokes the decision point, so nothing records anything — the stream is merely shorter. Implication: absence of denial records is not evidence of absence of action.
3. Declaring a producer and referencing a signature are affordances for accepting external evidence, not independence. Tier 3 needs: authenticated producer identity, verified attestation, protected capture, deployment binding, independent logging, demonstrated coverage.
4. Draft-only: consequence bounded by the human sender, adversary is accident — Tier 2 on drafting surfaces is defensible. Automatic sending: direct external effect — Tier 3 on the sending surface. Grade the method, not the conclusion.
5. Grade structure: named surfaces and revision, named mechanism and tier ("cooperative, Tier 2, declared surfaces only"), scoped evidence ("supplied, validating against schema S"), explicit "not claimed" clause.
6. Tier 3 buys independence, loses expressiveness: properties over rich governance vocabulary (approval bindings, delegation bounds, provenance obligations) are checkable at Tier 1 over artifacts but invisible to a network-level enforcement point. Tiers name what defeats a claim, not quality.

### Chapter 14 — Bypass, Coverage, and Negative Controls

1. Two states cannot separate "authors declined to wrap — deployer must avoid or externally control" (unsupported: the async surface) from "the integrator owns this" (unwrapped: graph construction). One label loses who owes what.
2. Strongest: the list is a menu of unguarded doors. Counters: capable attackers derive the gaps from code anyway; defenders cannot compensate for gaps they do not know; the list makes the positive claim falsifiable. Falsifiability (or derivability) usually persuades the security officer — credit the justification.
3. The test runs on every change, so the statement stays true or the build fails; and it pins exact behavior (the direct call runs ungoverned) rather than a paraphrase that drifts.
4. "Denied" requires: decision code asserted, the recorded denial pair present, side-effect ledger unchanged. Each catches a defect: wrong-reason denials, enforcement without recording, exception-after-effect.
5. Pinned: import fails loudly; the inventory and claim stay true; the upgrade becomes a governance event priced at a test run. Ranged: the new entry point loads silently, the inventory is incomplete, and "tool invocations are governed" is silently false.
6. A precise rewrite carries surface (the wrapped query path), mechanism (cooperative in-process denial), conditions (calls traversing the wrapper, revision R), and tier (Tier 2; direct connections out of scope). Grade all four elements and the identified qualifying words.

### Chapter 15 — Testing Governance Claims

1. The rewrite binds surface (specific egress paths), mechanism (the evaluating component), condition (coverage, revision). Five tests: allow (recorded before proceeding), deny (refused with code, no effect), failure (injected outage → no action), bypass (direct path pinned as declared), evidence (records validate and bind) — each with the observation that would falsify.
2. Recording after permits a window where the action ran with no decision on record; a crash there yields an effect without evidence. The passing defect: a wrapper that executes first and back-fills the record.
3. A conformance suite is written against the contract, portable across implementations, and defines required failure behavior. It matters more here because multiple adapters claim the same guarantees, and the suite makes "wrapped" mean one thing.
4. Scenario: malformed instant arrives after an approval expired; the clock fallback allows what should have been refused. The evidence then misrepresents the evaluation instant — the recorded decision looks properly time-bound when the time was invented.
5. Precisely: composed A-and-B never allows what A alone denies — narrowing only; added permissions cannot defeat superior prohibitions. Violating-but-clean-in-review: per-layer files look fine, but the merge resolves conflicts by precedence or last-writer-wins.
6. By Chapter 3's layers: the decision layer worked (no wrong allow) but evidence binding was defective, so "and we can demonstrate it" fails. Harmed: whoever needs the demonstration — auditor, risk officer, customer.

### Chapter 16 — Nornyx in Context

1. Tier 1 by construction (accept Tier 2, cooperative and scoped, where adapters are included). The preventing property is categorical: Nornyx executes and intercepts nothing, so no artifact of it can be the mandatory enforcement point Tier 3 requires.
2. `nornyx: "0.1"` names the contract document-format version; the 1.0 designation names the stability of the overall surface. The conclusion confuses a format marker with project maturity.
3. Examples must match badge semantics: implemented — code and tests at the snapshot (lock verification); guidance — documented target architecture (documentation-MCP pattern); extension — the book's design (hierarchy engine). Presenting guidance as implemented records a nonexistent control, and every downstream risk rating inherits the false premise.
4. Any schema-constant example (fixed protocol execution mode; generation-time rejection of endpoints/credentials). Structural enforcement makes the violation inexpressible in any document; documentation depends on every author reading and honoring it.
5. Both claim things about "the software" with no fixed referent. Failure: twelve months later, a verified control has changed or vanished, and the re-assessment either falsely re-affirms or re-derives everything a pin would have made checkable.
6. Counter at strength: the no-go proves defects existed, and only the vendor says they were fixed. Deciding information: itemized remediation with finding identifiers, the disclosed adjacent bypass, re-test evidence at a pinned revision — whether the statements are independently checkable.

### Chapter 17 — The Nornyx Policy Model

1. The normalizer yields one deny rule; `block` is unrecognized, so `block external_publish` silently becomes a *requirement*. The evaluator applies the deny at planning time via the keyword matcher; the requirement is never evaluated — a pending-evidence obligation. The author intended two prohibitions and got one.
2. The coarse five-category keyword matcher maps publish/release vocabulary into the same category as production writes — a category match, not meaning. An evading dangerous step: one worded outside the five categories (e.g., neutral data-movement wording). Lesson: coverage is keyword-shaped; stronger enforcement must inspect action semantics.
3. Unknown top-level block: warning (closed but tolerant top level). Unknown graph relation: warning (possibly forward-compatible vocabulary). Known relation, wrong endpoint kinds: error (typed relation; a mistyped edge is a contradiction).
4. True form: "the contract *records* origin, authority rank, and taint as hashed provenance metadata, making untrusted origin declared and reviewable." The original needs an execution-path enforcement component consulting the ranking at decision time.
5. Non-zero exit with a reference-resolution error — fail-closed. Preferable because resolving to an empty rule set would validate the contract while the intended baseline silently vanished.
6. Per-step replication shows an auditor exactly which obligations attach to each governed step; extracted records stay self-describing. Misleading when read as evaluation: nine pending-evidence entries can look like nine enforced checks when none is ever evaluated.

### Chapter 18 — Profiles, Modules, Locks, and Digests

1. Six, in dependency order: `evidence_integrity`, `human_approval`, `separation_of_duties`, `exception_management`, `change_control`, `architecture_conformance` — the transitive closure through `change_control`. Selecting one control concern imposes the whole chain's obligations; selection is binding, not decorative.
2. A timestamp makes identical inputs yield differing locks, so reproduction cannot verify. Legitimate external timestamp: commit metadata or CI logs recording when the lock was produced.
3. Failing configuration: a pipeline treating only exit 1 as failure (or gating on `check` alone), so the exit-2 integrity failure passes. Fix: fail on any non-zero exit.
4. Suspicious: a digest changes only because bound content changed. Legitimacy requires the changed content and regenerated artifacts in the same pull request. The question tooling cannot answer: was the change *authorized* — locks prove integrity, never authorship or approval.
5. Mechanism: the lock binds per-record digests and every artifact's SHA-256; the `purpose` string flows into rendered outputs, so all ten hashes change. Either verdict is creditable if defended: strength (one check catches any change in the set) versus usability (a hash wall for a one-word edit; `AN_LOCK_*` localization partly mitigates).
6. (a) Supported. (b) Unsupported — anyone with repository access regenerates a consistent lock; authenticity needs signing/branch protection/review. (c) Unsupported — locks say nothing about runtime behavior. (d) Partly — supported for verified digests, conditional on the framework actually reading those files.

### Chapter 19 — The Authorization SPI

1. Temporal: interpretations from different moments — shorter caches help (bound the window). Procedural: different code paths derive different conclusions — caching irrelevant. Assurance: components differ on whether validation ever ran — only a single assured construction path helps.
2. Paths let the interface own the whole pipeline in fixed order, so possession of an assured authorizer implies the pipeline ran; pre-parsed input would let callers skip stages. The plain constructor serves tests and exploration where no assurance is claimed — and saying so is honest.
3. Consumers: the internal mapping invites representation coupling and attempted mutation; detached copies let them read and even mutate without touching decisions or digests. Serialization: frozen internals may not round-trip as plain data. Decision: keep the copy — memory for unweakenable claims.
4. Only the narrow structural case: a declared crossing into an external zone requiring approval, presented without an accompanying assertion. Narrowness matters because a general "undecidable" outcome would erode default-deny.
5. Ordered binding checks establish the record is about this action, actor, and revision before the grant flag is read, making the failure code diagnostic. Reversed, a misbound record's "not granted" tells the operator "approval refused" when the record never applied — they seek re-approval instead of investigating.
6. Ask: is the authorizer constructed via `load_authorizer` (paths plus validation instant), or the assurance-free plain constructor? And is that interpretation the only one — or does the integration re-read contract content elsewhere, reintroducing split brain?

### Chapter 20 — Evidence Architecture

1. Forbidden so a stream is never half-migrated: optional occurrence identity would let one stream mix modes, and a reader could not tell whether an occurrence-less event is out-of-model work or a forgetful producer — e.g., a retry misread as new work.
2. Explicit mode has occurrence/attempt identity to distinguish repeated work, so `timestamp` becomes transport — restamped duplicates no longer evade. Legacy mode has no such identity, so the timestamp is all that separates two legitimate identical events; including it permits repetition but lets restamped replays through.
3. Attempts 1 and 3 trip the contiguity diagnostic: the stream has *told* the reviewer an attempt happened unrecorded. Attempt 1 alone of three validates: contiguity exposes omitted middles, nothing exposes omitted tails or streams.
4. An extracted event stays self-describing and checkable against the contract, lock, and revision it names, independent of its envelope. Cost: per-event size and a redundancy that must itself be checked for envelope consistency.
5. Only a grant transfers authority and accountability, so the non-human refusal binds `approval_granted`; a rejection confers nothing. The narrow scope makes automated rejections recordable — e.g., the engine's structural refusal of a non-human approval attempt.
6. Lenient default: report-generating runs are useful without breaking builds; the report records the failure. Strict case: a gate exiting zero on failure controls nothing. Resolution: pipeline authors must pass `--strict` wherever validation gates; the burden sits there because only the pipeline knows whether the invocation is a gate or a report.

### Chapter 21 — Diagnostics and Generated Artifacts

1. Without gating: divergence detectable but inconsequential — hand-edits accumulate. Without determinism: the gate fires on every regeneration and gets disabled. Without labeling: humans editing "what runs" get no signal that authority lives elsewhere, and review targets the wrong file.
2. A README-list gate compares only seven files: artifacts outside the list can be edited, stray files introduced, unlisted artifacts left unguarded. The manifest makes the inventory the machine's own record of what it wrote — covering changed, missing, and stray.
3. `--as-of` is the validation instant — expiries and validity are checked against it — so it decides whether generation may succeed; artifacts carry no time, so successful outputs are byte-identical. The flag sits in validation, upstream of rendering.
4. For: real documentation URLs are legitimate authoring needs. Against: the guarantee — declarations cannot carry endpoints, credentials, commands, URLs, IPs — is what makes artifacts inert for consumers; a warning turns inexpressible into discouraged. The answer should turn on artifact class (peer-consumed versus human-facing).
5. Expressible: "tolerate exactly diagnostic X, fail on everything else" — stable codes make the allowlist precise; numbers renumber and prose rewords. A rename silently changes every gating pipeline — codes are interface, renames are breaking.
6. Principle: sync repairs divergence from the canonical source; creating a missing policy block is authorship, an editorial decision. Wanted opposite: bootstrapping new members — made safe only by explicit opt-in plus review, so creation is a visible attributable act.

### Chapter 22 — Designing an Adapter Boundary

1. It breaks the closed declarative binding: the surface-to-capability mapping would be built from caller-controlled arguments. Attack: untrusted retrieved content steers the planner to supply a weaker capability reference — capability selection becomes an injection surface.
2. `enforce` discharges evaluate-once, record-before-acting, execute-only-on-allow; it delegates outcome and failure recording because observing "returned" versus "raised" is framework-specific. ## 3. Exercise guidance, by part

**Part I (Chapters 1–4).** All exercises are paper designs: inventories, classifications, decompositions, and rewrites; none requires the `nornyx` CLI, and most benefit from being run against a system the student actually knows. Common pitfalls: enumerating the reachable set at the level of "tools" rather than credentials, filesystem scope, and network capability (Chapter 1); writing remediation plans when the exercise asks for diagnosis (Chapter 2); and decomposing claims into restatements rather than genuinely narrower claims with distinct evidence producers (Chapter 3). A strong submission is concrete and falsifiable: named artifacts, named components, and explicit "I could not determine X" entries. Instructors should grade the honesty of the gaps as heavily as the completeness of the inventories — the Part I exercises exist to teach students that most environments cannot answer these questions, and a submission with no gaps usually means the student stopped looking.

**Part II (Chapters 5–10).** Predominantly paper: identity records, delegation designs, zone models, decision tables, approval-record and fatigue analyses. Two exercises need tooling: Chapter 8's workspace exercise requires the pinned `nornyx` CLI (`workspace-check`, `--write`), and Chapter 10's wrapper exercise requires Python (any test framework; no Nornyx dependency). Pitfalls: designing delegations without all four bounds (attenuation, depth, expiry, scope); zone models whose never-share lists are empty or decorative; and Chapter 10 wrappers whose tests assert exceptions rather than absence of effects. A strong submission states, for every design, the validation errors it must produce when edited carelessly — the mark of a student who has internalized that declarations are worth only what checks them. Fatigue-budget arithmetic (Chapter 9) should show its numbers.

**Part III (Chapters 11–15).** Mixed. Paper: evidence contracts, fingerprint designs, tier maps, claim registers, coverage inventories, claim rewrites. Toolchain: Chapter 11's evidence-import probe and Chapter 12's deterministic-generation/lock-tamper experiment require the pinned CLI; Chapter 15's failure-injection exercise needs a codebase the student can break, and its five-test sorting exercise needs an existing test suite. Pitfalls: treating the five tests per surface as five degrees of thoroughness rather than five distinct falsifiers; tier maps that assign tiers to systems instead of surfaces; and claim-register entries with empty "not claimed" lists. Strong submissions quote observed diagnostic codes and exit codes exactly (never paraphrased) and, in the failure-injection table, flag every row where observed behavior diverges from the control's description — divergences found are the point, not a defect of the submission.

**Part IV (Chapters 16–21).** The toolchain part: nearly every exercise requires the pinned Nornyx release installed locally (contract authoring with `check` and `policy-check`, generation, profiles and network locks, tamper-and-verify cycles, drift gates, forbidden-content scans). Exercises are offline and local by design; no network or external services are needed. Pitfalls: running against an unpinned Nornyx version and then being confused by diagnostic differences; editing generated artifacts instead of contracts and misreading the resulting drift; forgetting `--as-of` and misattributing validation failures; and reading `require` rules as enforcement when recording deliberate-breakage results. A strong submission is a lab notebook: exact commands, exact codes, exit statuses, and a short interpretation for each observation distinguishing what the tool proved from what it merely reported. The deliberate-breakage tables (Chapters 17–18) should classify every failure as caught-as-error, caught-as-warning, or not caught.

**Part V (Chapters 22–27).** The most environment-heavy part. Chapters 23–24 exercises need Python with the adapter distribution and the exactly pinned framework versions (CrewAI, LangGraph) — instructors should pre-build an environment, because the exact pins are themselves a lesson that costs class time. Chapter 22's evaluation matrix, Chapter 24's stream-prediction, Chapter 25's obligations design, and Chapter 26's projection specification and outage runbook are paper. Chapter 27's package scan needs the CLI and a real third-party bundle (scanned, never installed or executed — enforce this rule explicitly). Pitfalls: testing denials by exception rather than by ledger/side-effect absence; claiming coverage the inventory does not support; and writing projection specs that quietly compile away the untranslatable fields. Strong submissions attach the coverage claim each artifact supports, in Chapter 14's qualified form, to every deliverable.

**Part VI (Chapters 28–33).** Requires the CLI plus a CI environment the student can modify (any runner; the repository's reference pipeline is the model). Chapters 28–29 build authoring and pipeline lanes: fixtures asserting decision codes, three-diff review instrumentation, drift and lock gates routed by the exit-code contract. Chapters 30–32 are mostly paper against real organizational material — decision tables, gate write-ups in the eight-element format, platform-settings verification, workspace and conflict-classifier designs; Chapter 31's delegation-breaking and Chapter 32's two-level workspace need the CLI. Chapter 33 mixes signal instrumentation with evidence-preservation labwork. Pitfalls: pipelines that treat exit 1 and exit 2 identically; gate write-ups whose negative-test and bypass elements are left empty; exercising `--write` without predicting what sync will delete. Strong submissions run their own negative tests and report what the bypass attempt actually found, including "nothing, and here is why that is suspicious."

**Part VII (Chapters 34–38).** Almost entirely paper, aimed at the student's real environment: threat-tree extensions, mitigation registers, standards-mapping rebuilds, audit-chain walks, maturity and cost-ledger assessments, limitation tables. Chapter 34's tree extension and Chapter 38's falsification attempt are the exceptions needing the repository at the pinned snapshot. Pitfalls: registers with empty residual-risk cells or unowned rows; mapping rows rebuilt clause-first instead of control-first; audit worksheets that quietly fill gaps instead of reporting empty rows; and falsification write-ups that soften a reproduction into a "note." A strong submission names owners and dates for everything it hands to another team, reports ratios and findings rather than volume, and — for Chapter 38 — treats a failed falsification attempt as a verified claim with the write-up as its evidence artifact, exactly as the exercise instructs.

**Part VIII (Chapters 39–41).** The capstone part: exercises require the full local toolchain (contracts, workspace, generation, locks, evidence validation) and, for the adapter-wired injections, the Part V environment; the register, design-extension, and proposal exercises are paper. Chapter 39's exercises extend the design (new register rows, a fourth application, a cross-thread seam); Chapter 40's re-run the failure-injection programme and reconstruction; Chapter 41's produce cost ledgers and the Tier 3 proposal. Pitfalls: extending the design by modifying existing contracts when the exercise forbids it; injections reported without the fourfold deliverable (boundary, diagnostic family, preserved evidence, lesson); reconstructions that quietly consult the builder — every out-of-band question is a missing binding and must be reported as one. A strong submission's register rows are auditable sentences: scope, producer, tier, residual, each testable by a stranger. Grade against Section 4's rubric.

## 4. Capstone assessment criteria

The Part VIII capstone asks students to produce what Chapters 39–41 model: an integrated design for the Northstar estate (or an equivalent system of the student's own), a claim register, a working build with failure-injection transcripts, and an audit reconstruction. The rubric below grades seven dimensions. Weight them roughly equally, with one governing rule stated first because it overrides local scoring.

**The overclaiming rule.** The book's standard, established in Chapter 34 and enforced throughout Part VIII, is that an inflated claim is a vulnerability, not a style problem: broad unsupported claims lose credit *even when the accompanying code works*. A capstone whose build is flawless but whose register says "the executor cannot submit payments" without the "on evaluated paths" qualifier has failed the discipline the capstone exists to assess. Deduct for every claim that exceeds its mechanism, its coverage, or its tier, regardless of build quality; do not restore the credit because a demonstration succeeded. Conversely, an honestly scoped claim with a stated residual never loses credit for the residual's existence — only for its concealment.

**1. Explicit authority.** *Excellent:* a consolidated inventory of identities, capabilities, zones, and memberships in which every authority-bearing relation is a declared record with bounds and intervals, and deliberate absences (unheld capabilities, unapprovable identities) are declared and checkable rather than implicit. *Adequate:* the inventory covers all agents and capabilities but relations (delegations, memberships) are partly implicit, or absences are asserted in prose rather than declared. *Inadequate:* authority must be inferred from code, tool wiring, or narrative; the reachable and authorized sets are not distinguishable from the submission.

**2. Proportional control.** *Excellent:* tier and gate selection walked per action class from consequence and adversary, with low-consequence surfaces deliberately left lightly governed and the reasoning shown; no gate exists that the consequence analysis does not justify. *Adequate:* high-consequence surfaces are correctly gated but the analysis is implicit or uniform — everything gets the same treatment regardless of consequence. *Inadequate:* control placement is decorative (heavy gates on trivial actions, none on irreversible ones) or tier selection ignores the adversary actually in scope.

**3. Claim–evidence traceability.** *Excellent:* every register row names its enforcing mechanism, its evidence artifacts (by digest or path), and its supporting tests or transcripts; a reader can traverse from any claim sentence to the artifact that supports it without asking the author. *Adequate:* rows name mechanisms and evidence classes but some links are generic ("CI logs") rather than specific artifacts. *Inadequate:* claims are supported by narrative, demonstration recollection, or the build's general existence.

**4. Honest coverage and status labeling.** *Excellent:* wrapped, unsupported, and unwrapped surfaces are inventoried with reasons; every design element is correctly labeled **[implemented]**, **[guidance]**, or **[extension]**; the design passes the deletion test — remove every extension and every remaining claim stays true. *Adequate:* the inventory exists but a few obligations are misassigned (unsupported versus unwrapped), or one extension quietly props up a current-tense claim. *Inadequate:* coverage is asserted per system rather than per surface, or roadmap machinery is presented as deployed.

**5. Fail-closed behavior.** *Excellent:* failure injection attacks the design's own boundaries (drift, lock tamper, stale approval, replay, identity duplication, artifact removal, and the deliberate bypass), each with the fourfold deliverable — boundary, diagnostic family, preserved evidence, lesson — and the bypass that succeeds is *predicted* by the register. *Adequate:* injections run but deliverables are partial (diagnostics quoted without preserved evidence), or a failure mode surprises the register without the register being corrected. *Inadequate:* only happy-path demonstration; or a gate that fails open is discovered and left undiscussed.

**6. Audit reconstructability.** *Excellent:* a consequential decision is reconstructed through the full chain — contract, composition, lock, approval, events, artifacts, interpretation — from preserved artifacts alone, with the conclusion carrying its scope block; an assessor (or a colleague, per the Chapter 40 exercise) can repeat it without out-of-band questions. *Adequate:* the chain is traversable but requires the author's help at one or two steps, and those gaps are acknowledged. *Inadequate:* reconstruction depends on regenerated artifacts, dashboards, or memory; or the conclusion outruns its worksheet.

**7. Cost and limitation awareness.** *Excellent:* the submission carries a cost ledger (authoring effort, pin maintenance, approval latency, expertise) and a limitations statement in Chapter 38's form — each limitation paired with the compensating control someone must supply — plus a defensible judgment about where the machinery is *not* warranted. *Adequate:* costs and limits are listed but not connected to obligations or decisions. *Inadequate:* the submission presents governance as free, or its limitations section is boilerplate that names no transferred obligation.

A borderline submission is best resolved by the register: read only the claim register and its residual column, then check three rows at random against the build. If the rows survive, the student has learned the discipline; if they inflate, no quantity of working code compensates.

## 5. Recommended learning paths

**Software engineer new to governance.** Read linearly through Part I, then 5, 7, 9, 10 from Part II; 11, 13, 14, 15 from Part III; all of Part IV at a keyboard; then 22–25, 28–30. Rationale: engineers profit most from reaching the toolchain quickly, but Chapters 13–15 must precede it or the tooling reads as features rather than claims. Return for 6, 8, 12, and Part VII before attempting the capstone.

**Enterprise architect.** 1–4, 6, 8, 10, 13, 16, 18, 26, 31–33, 37, 39, 41. Rationale: the architect's questions are placement, composition, and hierarchy — enforcement models, composition and locks, external enforcement points, the Charter thread, and adoption strategy — with the capstone design chapter as the worked template. Chapters 22–25 can be skimmed for the coverage vocabulary.

**Security engineer.** 3, 5, 6, 10, 12, 13, 14, 15, then 19–21, 23 (for the bypass test), 26, 27, 34, and 38. Rationale: the discipline's security content concentrates in authority confusion, bypass and coverage, negative controls, threat modeling, and the parser and lock hardening of Chapter 34; Chapters 7–9 should still be read before writing any policy, since approval semantics are where security reviews of these systems most often go wrong.

**Governance, risk, or audit professional.** 1–4, 9, 11, 13, 14, 16 (for status labels), 18 (for locks, lightly), 28, 33, 35, 36, 37, 39–40 (register and reconstruction sections). Rationale: this path builds the ability to read claims, evidence packages, coverage inventories, and registers critically without operating the toolchain; Chapter 36 is the professional core, and Chapter 35's seven-field row structure transfers directly to existing mapping work.

**Platform or SRE.** 4, 10, 12, 13, 15, 16, 18, 19, 21, 22, 25, 26, 29, 33, 37. Rationale: this role owns enforcement placement, pipelines, determinism, diagnostics-as-interface, exit-code routing, and the operational health of the governance layer itself; Chapters 29 and 33 are the center of gravity, with Chapter 26 framing the platform enforcement points the role already operates.

**Researcher or graduate student.** The full linear read is the intended path; where time is short, prioritize 3, 7, 12, 13, 14, 20, 24, 31, 34, 38, with 38's open problems and research directions as the bridge to the literature. Chapter 15's property-based framing and Chapter 12's identity hierarchy repay close reading.

**Policy author.** 6, 7, 8, 9, 17, 18, 28, 32, then 14 and 35. Rationale: this path runs from policy semantics through composition and approvals into the concrete authoring workflow and the hierarchy thread; Chapter 28 is the daily-work chapter, and Chapters 14 and 35 teach the author to write rules and mappings whose claims survive review. Chapter 38's account of the rule language's limits is mandatory before writing production contracts.
